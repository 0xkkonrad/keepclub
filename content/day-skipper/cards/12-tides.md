# 12 Tides and tidal heights

## What causes tides?

The gravitational pull of the moon and, to a lesser extent, the sun on the oceans, plus the centrifugal effect of the earth-moon system. The moon dominates, so the tidal day is about 24 h 50 min and there are usually two high waters a day, roughly 12 h 25 min apart.

## What are spring and neap tides, and when do they occur?

<b>Springs</b> — the sun and moon pull in line at new and full moon, giving the largest range: highest highs and lowest lows.<br><b>Neaps</b> — at the first and last quarter the pulls are at right angles, giving the smallest range. There is a lag of about two days after the phase of the moon.

## Define chart datum and say what UK charts use.

The level from which charted depths and drying heights are measured. On UK Admiralty charts it is approximately <b>Lowest Astronomical Tide (LAT)</b>, the lowest level predictable under average meteorological conditions, so the sea very rarely falls below the charted depth.

![diagram](ds-tidal-datums.png)

## What are MHWS, MHWN, MLWN and MLWS?

Mean High Water Springs, Mean High Water Neaps, Mean Low Water Neaps and Mean Low Water Springs — the average heights above chart datum of high and low waters at springs and neaps.

![diagram](ds-tidal-datums.png)

## What is the height of tide, and what is the range?

The <b>height of tide</b> is the height of the sea surface above chart datum at a given moment. The <b>range</b> is the difference between the heights of successive high and low waters.

![diagram](ds-tidal-datums.png)

## What is the depth of water at a place?

Charted depth + height of tide.

![diagram](ds-tidal-datums.png)

## How much water is there over a patch that dries 1.8 m when the height of tide is 4.2 m?

Height of tide &minus; drying height = 4.2 &minus; 1.8 = <b>2.4 m</b>.

![diagram](ds-tidal-datums.png)

## How do you work out clearance under the keel?

Charted depth + height of tide &minus; draught. Then subtract a safety margin for swell, squat, chart accuracy and barometric effect before you decide to go.

![diagram](ds-tidal-datums.png)

## What datum are charted heights and vertical clearances measured from?

Heights of <b>lights, beacons and land features</b> are above <b>MHWS</b>. <b>Vertical clearances</b> under bridges and overhead cables are above <b>HAT</b> on new-edition Admiralty charts and MHWS on older ones — the title block says which. Real clearance = charted clearance + the height datum &minus; height of tide, which is normally <b>more</b> than the charted figure.

![diagram](ds-tidal-datums.png)

## State the rule of twelfths.

Between low and high water the tide rises by 1, 2, 3, 3, 2 then 1 twelfths of the range in each successive sixth of the tidal duration.

![diagram](ds-rule-of-twelfths.png)

## LW 1.2 m at 0600, HW 5.4 m at 1215. Estimate the height at 0900.

Range 5.4 &minus; 1.2 = 4.2 m, so a twelfth is 0.35 m. Duration 6 h 15 min, so each sixth is 62&frac12; min. 0900 is 180 min after LW, which is 2.9 sixths — call it 3. Risen 1+2+3 = 6 twelfths = 2.1 m, so the height is 1.2 + 2.1 = <b>about 3.3 m</b> above chart datum.

![diagram](ds-rule-of-twelfths.png)

## What are the limitations of the rule of twelfths?

It assumes a symmetrical, roughly sinusoidal curve and a six-hour rise. It is unreliable where the curve is distorted — the Solent and Poole with their double high waters, the Thames, and many rivers and estuaries. For anything tight use the tidal curve.

![diagram](ds-rule-of-twelfths.png)

## Describe how you use a standard port tidal curve to find the height at a given time.

Take HW and LW times and heights from the tables.<br>Fill in the hour boxes with HW time in the centre.<br>Draw the sloping line joining the LW height on the lower scale to the HW height on the upper scale.<br>Enter the graph at the time wanted, go up to the curve (spring, neap, or interpolated by range), across to the sloping line, then up or down to the height scale — the top and bottom scales are the same graduation.<br>Check the diagram's reference: a few ports, including Portland, Poole and Christchurch, have curves drawn about <b>LW</b>, so the centre box is LW time.

![diagram](ds-tidal-curve.png)

## How do you use the tidal curve the other way — to find the time at which a given height occurs?

Enter at the height on the sloping line, go up (or down) to the curve, then across to the time scale. Do it for both the rise and the fall to get the window during which you have enough water.

![diagram](ds-tidal-curve.png)

## Which curve do you use when the range is between springs and neaps?

Interpolate between the spring and neap curves in proportion to the day's actual range compared with the mean spring and mean neap ranges printed on the diagram.

![diagram](ds-tidal-curve.png)

## What is a standard port and what is a secondary port?

A <b>standard port</b> has its own full tide tables and tidal curve. A <b>secondary port</b> is worked out from a nominated standard port by applying tabulated <b>time differences</b> and <b>height differences</b> from the almanac.

## How do you work out HW time and height at a secondary port?

Take the standard port's HW time and height.<br>The table gives time differences at two tabulated HW times: interpolate between them on the standard port's <b>actual HW time</b>.<br>It gives height differences at the standard port's MHWS and MHWN (and MLWN and MLWS): interpolate on the standard port's <b>actual height</b>.<br>Apply both differences.<br>Then work the height on the <b>standard port's curve</b>, with the secondary port's HW time in the centre box and its own HW and LW heights on the scales, unless the almanac prints a curve for the secondary port itself.

## What does 'the tide is making' or 'the tide is taking off' mean?

<b>Making</b> — the ranges are increasing day by day towards springs.<br><b>Taking off</b> — the ranges are decreasing towards neaps.

## What is the difference between a tidal gate and a tidal window?

A <b>tidal gate</b> is a place you can only pass at certain states of tide — a race, an overfall, a bridge, a sill, a bar. A <b>tidal window</b> is the span of time during which you have enough depth or a fair stream to do so. Plan the passage around the gates.

## What is a sill, and what must you check before entering a marina with one?

A wall across the entrance that retains water inside at low tide. Check the height of the sill above chart datum,<br>your draught,<br>and the height of tide at your arrival and departure times,<br>and remember you may be locked in as well as out.

## What effects can make the actual tide differ from the prediction?

<b>Barometric pressure</b> — roughly 0.01 m per millibar of departure from <b>1013 mb</b>, so 30 mb below it raises the level about 0.3 m and a high depresses it; <b>strong or prolonged wind</b> can pile water up on a coast (a surge) or hold it back; heavy river flow raises levels in an estuary. Never plan on the last 0.2 m.

## Why is the ebb usually more dangerous than the flood for a small yacht?

The ebb runs out against the prevailing swell over shoals and bars, steepening and breaking the seas, and it takes you offshore away from shelter if you break down. Many harbour bars are only safe on the flood.

## Where do you find the time zone of a set of tide tables, and why does it matter?

In the heading of the table — UK Admiralty tables are in <b>UT (GMT)</b>. In British Summer Time you must <b>add one hour</b> to every tabulated time. Getting this wrong is the single most common tidal error.

## Which tidal curve do you use for a secondary port?

The <b>standard port's</b> curve, unless the almanac prints one for the secondary port itself. Work out the secondary port's HW and LW times and heights first, then put its HW time in the centre box and its own HW and LW heights on the height scales. Choose the spring or neap curve, or interpolate, on the <b>standard port's</b> range.

![diagram](ds-tidal-curve.png)

## You draw 1.8 m and want 1.0 m under the keel to cross a bar charted at 0.6 m. What height of tide do you need, and how do you find the window?

Required height of tide = draught + clearance &minus; charted depth = 1.8 + 1.0 &minus; 0.6 = <b>2.2 m</b>. Enter the tidal curve at 2.2 m on the sloping line, read across to the curve and down to the time scale on both the rise and the fall: the span between those two times is your <b>tidal window</b>.

![diagram](ds-tidal-curve.png)

## What are LAT and HAT, and where does each appear?

<b>Lowest</b> and <b>Highest Astronomical Tide</b> — the extreme levels predictable under average meteorological conditions and any combination of astronomical conditions. Chart datum is at approximately <b>LAT</b>, so charted depths are almost never overstated. Vertical clearances under bridges and cables on new-edition Admiralty charts are above <b>HAT</b>. Neither is a limit the weather cannot beat.

![diagram](ds-tidal-datums.png)
