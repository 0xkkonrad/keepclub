# 17 Pilotage

## What is the difference between navigation and pilotage?

<b>Navigation</b> is getting from A to B in open water using the chart, the tide and the compass, with time to plot. <b>Pilotage</b> is close-quarters work near the shore where there is no time to plot: you work from a prepared plan, marks, transits, depths and eyeball.

![diagram](ds-fix-pilotage.png)

## What goes into a pilotage plan?

A simple sketch you can read one-handed in the dark:<br>the legs with courses and distances,<br>the buoys and lights in the order you will see them with their characteristics,<br>transits and leading lines,<br>clearing bearings for each hazard,<br>a depth contour to stay outside,<br>the tidal height needed and when,<br>waypoints,<br>and an <b>abort plan</b>.

![diagram](ds-fix-pilotage.png)

## Why is a transit better than a compass bearing?

It has <b>no error at all</b> — no variation, no deviation, no reading error — and it shows you instantly whether you are drifting off the line. Two charted objects in line is the most reliable pilotage tool there is.

![diagram](ds-fix-pilotage.png)

## What is a leading line?

A charted line, usually defined by two marks or two lights in transit, that leads safely into a harbour or through a channel. The chart gives its bearing; the marks give you the accuracy.

![diagram](ds-fix-pilotage.png)

## What is a clearing bearing and how do you use it?

A bearing of a charted object which, if you keep the actual bearing greater than (or less than) it, keeps you clear of a danger. Written as 'keep the bearing of the church spire greater than 070&deg;', or NLT / NMT on the chart — a single number the helm can hold without looking at the chart.

![diagram](ds-fix-pilotage.png)

## How do you use the echo sounder as a safety line?

Choose a depth contour that clears the dangers, work out what the sounder should read at that contour for the height of tide at the time, and treat that number as a wall: if the depth falls to it, turn away. It works in fog and at night.

## What is a head bearing, and what is it good for?

The bearing of a mark you are heading towards. Watch it: if it stays constant you are making good the track towards the mark; if it changes, the tide is setting you off and you must alter.

## What is the practical rule about speed in pilotage?

Go slowly enough that you can keep ahead of the boat. If you cannot identify the next mark before you reach the last one, slow down, stop, or turn back onto a reciprocal course until you have sorted out where you are.

## What preparation should be done on deck before entering a harbour?

Fenders and warps rigged both sides,<br>boat hook to hand,<br>anchor ready to let go,<br>sails stowed or ready to drop,<br>engine warm and tested in ahead and astern,<br>crew briefed with jobs allocated,<br>and the pilotage plan and VHF channel to hand in the cockpit.

## Why brief the crew before the entrance rather than during it?

Because once you are in it you will be busy with the helm and the marks, and a shouted instruction in a confined channel is how boats get damaged. Allocate helm,<br>lookout,<br>fenders,<br>lines and log-keeper in advance.

## What information do you look up before entering an unfamiliar harbour?

Approach and entrance in the pilot book and almanac,<br>harbour plan,<br>tidal height and stream at your arrival time,<br>any bar, sill or lock and its operating hours,<br>port signals and traffic lights,<br>VHF working channel and whether you must call in,<br>visitor berths,<br>prohibited areas,<br>and the abort options if you cannot get in.

## What do port entry traffic signals typically mean?

Three <b>flashing</b> red lights in a vertical line = serious emergency, all vessels stop or divert as instructed (IPTS 1).<br>Three <b>fixed</b> reds = vessels shall not proceed (IPTS 2).<br>Red, white, red = do not proceed except outside the main channel (IPTS 3).<br>Three greens = proceed, one-way traffic;<br>green, white, green = proceed, two-way traffic. Local variations exist, so read the almanac entry for the specific port before you rely on it.

## What is 'IPTS'?

The International Port Traffic Signals, a standardised set of light signals controlling movement in and out of harbours, described in the almanac. A single yellow light alongside the main signal often exempts small craft — but only where the port says so.

## How do you handle pilotage at night?

Prepare everything in daylight,<br>preserve night vision with red light and no white deck lights,<br>identify each light by counting its period rather than guessing,<br>use the order of lights in your plan,<br>post a dedicated lookout free of other tasks,<br>and go slower than you would by day. Background shore lights make buoys very hard to pick out.

## Why are lit buoys so hard to spot on the approach to a lit town?

Because a 3-mile flashing buoy competes with streetlights, illuminated signs and moving car headlights ashore. Use transits,<br>leading lights,<br>depth and your prepared bearings, not just buoy-hopping.

## What is an abort plan and when do you decide to use it?

The pre-agreed route back out to safe water, with the course to steer and the depth you expect. Decide the <b>trigger</b> in advance —<br>cannot identify the leading marks,<br>too much wind across the entrance,<br>not enough depth,<br>traffic in the channel<br>— and act on it early rather than pressing on.

## How does the tidal stream inside a harbour entrance differ from outside?

It can reverse, accelerate through the gap, and set strongly across the entrance or along a pontoon face. Expect an eddy behind a breakwater running the opposite way to the main stream, and plan the last hundred metres for it.
