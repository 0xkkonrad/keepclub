# 02 Ropework and knots

## Figure of eight — use

A stopper knot in the end of a sheet or halyard so it cannot run out through a block or fairlead.

![diagram](ds-knots-ropes.png)

## Bowline — use

Makes a fixed loop that will not slip and can be untied after loading. Used to attach a sheet to a clew, drop a loop over a post, or make a temporary loop anywhere.

![diagram](ds-knots-ropes.png)

## Clove hitch — use and limitation

Attaches a line to a rail, post or ring — the classic fender lanyard knot. It can slip or work loose under a varying or uneven load, so it is not for a load-bearing mooring line.

## Round turn and two half hitches — use

Secures a mooring line to a ring, post or bollard. The round turn takes the load, so it can be tied and untied while the line is under tension.

## Rolling hitch — use

Grips along another rope or a spar. The standard fix for a riding turn on a winch or a jammed sheet: tie it to the loaded line and lead it to another winch to take the strain.

## Reef knot — correct use and the trap

Joins the two ends of the <b>same</b> line of equal diameter — tying in a reef, a sail tie, a lashing. It is <b>not</b> a knot for joining two ropes under load: use a sheet bend or a double sheet bend.

## Sheet bend and double sheet bend

Join two ropes together. Use the <b>double</b> sheet bend when the ropes differ in size or are slippery — the extra turn stops the thinner one pulling through.

## Which knot for a jib sheet to the clew of a headsail?

A bowline. It holds securely under load and, unlike most knots, can still be undone afterwards.

## What are the three parts of a rope you name when tying knots?

The <b>standing part</b> (the loaded main length),<br>the <b>bight</b> (a bend or loop in it),<br>and the <b>working end</b> (the end you tie with). The extreme end is the <b>tail</b>; strictly, the <b>bitter end</b> means the inboard end of the anchor cable, made fast at the bitts.

## Polyester (Terylene / Dacron) — properties and use

Low stretch, good UV and abrasion resistance, sinks, holds its strength when wet. The standard rope for sheets and halyards.

![diagram](ds-knots-ropes.png)

## Nylon (polyamide) — properties and use

Very strong and stretchy, so it absorbs shock loads. Used for mooring lines and anchor warps. It loses roughly 10-15% of its strength when wet and degrades in UV.

## Polypropylene — properties and use

Cheap, light, and it floats. Poor UV resistance and comparatively low strength, so it is for heaving lines, dinghy painters and lifebuoy lines, not for load-bearing work.

## Aramid and HMPE (Dyneema, Spectra) — properties and use

Extremely strong for their weight with almost no stretch, but expensive and slippery. Used for racing halyards, high-load running rigging and soft shackles.

## Why whip or heat-seal a rope's end?

To stop the rope unlaying or the braided cover milking off the core. A whipping is the seamanlike job; a heat-seal is quicker but brittle.

## How much strength does a knot cost you?

A knot puts a tight bend in the fibres and can cut a rope's breaking strength by roughly 30-50% — a bowline is often quoted at about 40% loss. A properly made splice loses much less, typically under 10%.

## Care of synthetic rope

Rinse off salt in fresh water,<br>dry before stowing,<br>keep out of sunlight,<br>avoid sharp bends and chafe,<br>never stand on a rope,<br>and retire it when the cover is furred or the core is stiff and glazed.

## How do you coil rope so it runs cleanly?

Laid (three-strand) rope is coiled clockwise with a slight twist in each turn. Braided rope prefers a figure-of-eight flake, which puts in no twist. A rope that has been coiled the wrong way will kink and jam in a block.

## Which knot takes the load off a jammed, loaded sheet?

A rolling hitch on the loaded line, led to a spare winch or cleat. Then you can clear the original turn safely.
