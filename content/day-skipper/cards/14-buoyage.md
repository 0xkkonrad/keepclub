# 14 IALA buoyage

## What is a port hand mark in IALA Region A?

<b>Red</b>, can (cylindrical) shape, with a red can topmark if any, showing a <b>red light of any rhythm except Fl(2+1)R</b>, which is reserved for the preferred-channel mark — for example Fl R 5s. Keep it to <b>port</b> when travelling in the direction of buoyage.

![diagram](ds-iala-lateral.png)

## What is a starboard hand mark in IALA Region A?

<b>Green</b>, conical shape, with a green cone topmark point up if any, showing a <b>green light of any rhythm except Fl(2+1)G</b>, which is reserved for the preferred-channel mark — for example Fl G 3s. Keep it to <b>starboard</b> in the direction of buoyage.

![diagram](ds-iala-lateral.png)

## What is the direction of buoyage?

The direction a vessel takes when approaching a harbour, river or estuary from seaward, or elsewhere the direction shown on the chart by a broad arrow symbol. The general convention is clockwise around continental land masses. <b>Around the British Isles it runs north up the west coast and through the Irish Sea, east through the English Channel, and north through the North Sea</b> — so it is not a clockwise circuit of the islands.

![diagram](ds-iala-lateral.png)

## How do the lateral colours differ between IALA Region A and Region B?

The colours are <b>reversed</b>: in Region B (the Americas, Japan, Korea, the Philippines) port hand is <b>green</b> and starboard hand is <b>red</b>. Shapes and topmarks are unchanged. 'Red right returning' is a Region B rule and is wrong in the UK.

![diagram](ds-iala-lateral.png)

## Describe a preferred channel to starboard mark in Region A.

<b>Red with a single broad green horizontal band</b>, can shape, light <b>Fl(2+1) R</b>. It marks a channel division; treat it as a <b>port hand</b> mark for the main channel, which lies to starboard of it.

![diagram](ds-iala-lateral.png)

## Describe a preferred channel to port mark in Region A.

<b>Green with a single broad red horizontal band</b>, conical shape, light <b>Fl(2+1) G</b>. Treat it as a <b>starboard hand</b> mark; the main channel lies to port of it.

![diagram](ds-iala-lateral.png)

## Describe the North cardinal mark.

<b>Black above yellow</b>. Topmark: two black cones both pointing <b>up</b>. Light: <b>Q</b> or <b>VQ</b>, white. Pass to the <b>north</b> of it — safe water lies north of the mark.

![diagram](ds-cardinal-marks.png)

## Describe the East cardinal mark.

<b>Black, yellow band, black</b>. Topmark: two black cones <b>base to base</b>. Light: <b>Q(3) 10s</b> or VQ(3) 5s, white. Pass to the <b>east</b> of it.

![diagram](ds-cardinal-marks.png)

## Describe the South cardinal mark.

<b>Yellow above black</b>. Topmark: two black cones both pointing <b>down</b>. Light: <b>Q(6) + LFl 15s</b> or VQ(6) + LFl 10s, white — the long flash confirms you counted six. Pass to the <b>south</b> of it.

![diagram](ds-cardinal-marks.png)

## Describe the West cardinal mark.

<b>Yellow, black band, yellow</b>. Topmark: two black cones <b>point to point</b>, like a wine glass. Light: <b>Q(9) 15s</b> or VQ(9) 10s, white. Pass to the <b>west</b> of it.

![diagram](ds-cardinal-marks.png)

## What is the memory aid for cardinal light rhythms?

A clock face: East is 3 o'clock so 3 flashes;<br>South is 6 o'clock so 6 flashes plus a long flash;<br>West is 9 o'clock so 9 flashes;<br>North is 12 o'clock so continuous quick flashing.

![diagram](ds-cardinal-marks.png)

## What are the cardinal quadrant boundaries?

NW, NE, SE and SW — true bearings 315&deg;, 045&deg;, 135&deg; and 225&deg;, measured <b>from the danger</b>.

![diagram](ds-cardinal-marks.png)

## Describe an isolated danger mark.

<b>Black with one or more broad red horizontal bands</b>, topmark <b>two black spheres</b> in a vertical line, light <b>Fl(2) white</b>. It is moored <b>on or above</b> a small isolated danger that has navigable water all round it.

![diagram](ds-other-marks.png)

## Describe a safe water mark.

<b>Red and white vertical stripes</b>,<br>spherical or with a single <b>red sphere</b> topmark,<br>light white: <b>Iso, Oc, LFl 10s or Mo(A)</b>. There is navigable water all round it. Often used as a mid-channel or landfall mark.

![diagram](ds-other-marks.png)

## Describe a special mark.

<b>Yellow</b>,<br>any shape not conflicting with a navigational mark,<br>topmark a single <b>yellow X</b>,<br>light <b>yellow</b>, in any rhythm <b>except those used for the white lights of cardinal, isolated danger and safe water marks</b>. It marks a special area or feature —<br>spoil ground,<br>outfall,<br>cables,<br>military exercise area,<br>recreation zone,<br>racing marks,<br>ODAS buoys.

![diagram](ds-other-marks.png)

## Describe the emergency wreck marking buoy.

<b>Blue and yellow vertical stripes</b>, topmark a yellow upright <b>cross</b>, light <b>alternating blue and yellow, Al Oc BuY 3s</b>. It is deployed on a newly discovered wreck until the wreck is charted and permanently marked.

![diagram](ds-other-marks.png)

## What is the difference between an isolated danger mark and a safe water mark in practice?

The isolated danger mark sits <b>on</b> the hazard — never pass close to it. The safe water mark has deep water all round and may be passed on either side.

![diagram](ds-other-marks.png)

## What does a yellow buoy with a yellow X topmark near an outfall tell you?

It is a special mark: not a navigational hazard mark in itself, but there is something there — a pipeline, cable or restricted area. Look it up on the chart before you cut inside it.

## How is a small-craft channel or a recommended track for yachts often marked?

By small buoys, sometimes yellow special marks, or by charted small-craft channels outside the main fairway. Harbour byelaws often require small craft to use them and to keep clear of the deep-water channel.

## What is a withy or a perch?

A local, unofficial or simple mark — a bundle of twigs or a pole — used in creeks and shallow channels. They may follow local convention rather than IALA and are not always charted, so treat them with care and read the pilot book.

## How can you tell a lit buoy from an unlit one on the chart?

A lit buoy carries a magenta flare symbol and the light's characteristic, e.g. 'Fl G 5s'. No flare means unlit — which matters enormously if you plan to arrive after dark.

## You see a buoy with a black body, a red band and two black spheres. What is it and what do you do?

An isolated danger mark: there is a hazard right there with navigable water round it. Identify it on the chart, work out which side gives you the room you need, and give it a wide berth.

![diagram](ds-other-marks.png)

## Which lights and marks does the IALA buoyage system NOT apply to?

Landfall lights,<br>leading lights and marks,<br>sector lights,<br>and major floating lights. They keep their own charted characteristics — so a red light in an approach is not necessarily a port-hand buoy.

## What is a 'new danger', and how is it marked?

An uncharted hazard — wreck, rock or shoal — not yet promulgated in nautical publications. It is marked with lateral, cardinal or isolated danger marks, or with an emergency wreck marking buoy. One of the marks may be <b>duplicated</b> until the danger is charted, and it may carry a racon showing Morse <b>D</b> and an AIS transponder.

![diagram](ds-other-marks.png)

## In Region B, what is a green buoy with a red band showing Fl(2+1) G?

A <b>preferred channel to starboard</b> mark. In Region B green is the port-hand colour, so the preferred-channel colours and rhythms swap over: Fl(2+1)G marks a preferred channel to starboard and Fl(2+1)R a preferred channel to port.

![diagram](ds-iala-lateral.png)

## What are the standard buoy shapes, and when does shape tell you nothing?

Can (cylindrical),<br>conical,<br>spherical,<br>pillar<br>and spar. On pillar and spar buoys — and on all fixed beacons — only the <b>topmark and colour</b> have navigational significance, because the body shape is dictated by the structure.

## Apart from which side to pass, what else can a cardinal mark be telling you?

Where the best navigable water lies;<br>the safe side on which to pass a danger;<br>or a feature in a channel — a bend, a junction, a branch, or the end of a shoal.
