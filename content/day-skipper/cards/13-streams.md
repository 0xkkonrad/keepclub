# 13 Tidal streams

## What is the difference between a tidal stream and a current?

A <b>tidal stream</b> is the horizontal flow of water caused by the tide; it reverses predictably. A <b>current</b> is a non-tidal flow driven by wind, river outflow or ocean circulation, and it does not reverse.

## Define set and drift, and rate.

<b>Set</b> is the direction the stream flows <b>towards</b>, in degrees true. <b>Rate</b> is its speed in knots. <b>Drift</b> is the distance the stream carries you over a stated period.

## How do you read a tidal diamond table?

Find the diamond letter on the chart, then in the almanac read the row for the hour relative to HW at the named reference port. It gives the set in degrees true, and the rate for springs and for neaps. Interpolate for the actual range.

## A tidal stream atlas arrow reads 19,34. What does that mean?

The rate is <b>1.9 knots at neaps and 3.4 knots at springs</b> in the direction of the arrow, for that hour relative to HW at the atlas's reference port.

## How do you get the actual rate when the day is neither springs nor neaps?

Interpolate between the neap and spring figures in proportion to the day's range compared with the mean neap and mean spring ranges. Many almanacs print a computation-of-rates diagram to do this graphically.

## Where do tidal streams run fastest?

Where the flow is squeezed: round headlands,<br>through narrows and channels,<br>over shallow banks,<br>and between islands. Streams are weaker in bays and close inshore, which is why you cheat the tide in the shallows.

## What is a tidal race and an overfall?

Disturbed, steep and breaking water where a strong stream runs over an uneven bottom or round a headland, or where two streams meet. It can be dangerous out of proportion to the wind strength. Avoid it, or time your passage for slack water and a fair wind.

## What happens when wind and tide oppose one another?

The waves become steeper, shorter and more likely to break — 'wind against tide'. The same wind with the tide gives a longer, easier sea. A gale against a spring ebb over a shoal is genuinely dangerous.

## What is a lee-bow tide and why do skippers like it?

A tidal stream setting onto the leeward bow. The tide's vector is the same whichever tack you are on, so it is not free windward gain — the point is that on the lee-bow tack the stream carries you <b>towards</b> your windward objective rather than away from it, and the boat sails a heading closer to the track you need. When beating, work the tack that puts the tide on the lee bow.

## What is slack water, and does it coincide with high water?

The short period when the stream is not running. It does <b>not</b> necessarily coincide with HW or LW: in some places the stream turns close to local high and low water, in others nearer mid-tide, and the difference can be two to three hours. Always take the timing from the tidal stream atlas or the diamond, referenced to HW at the named port — never guess it from the tide table.

## Why should you plan a passage around the tidal stream and not just the wind?

Because 2 knots of stream on a 5 knot boat is 40% of your speed: fair tide gives 7 knots over the ground, foul gives 3. Over a six-hour tide that is the difference between arriving and not.

## How do you work out the total tidal effect over a three-hour passage?

Take the set and rate for each of the three hours from the diamonds or atlas, and either plot them end to end as three successive vectors or resolve them into one resultant vector for the whole period. Then use that resultant in the course-to-steer triangle.

## What is the practical difference between working out a course to steer hour by hour and for the whole passage?

Hour by hour keeps you close to the intended track throughout, which matters if there are dangers either side. A single resultant for the whole leg gets you to the destination but lets you wander well off the straight line in between — fine in open water, wrong in confined water.

## Why does a strong tidal stream matter when you pick up a mooring or come alongside?

Because the boat steers by water flow, not ground speed. Approach into the stronger of wind and tide so you have steerage way at a low speed over the ground, and remember you will stop much faster with the tide against you than with it behind you.
