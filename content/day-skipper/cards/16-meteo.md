# 16 Meteorology

## What is the Beaufort scale, and what are forces 4, 6 and 8 called?

A scale of wind force from 0 to 12 based on observed sea state. Force 4 is a <b>moderate breeze</b>, 11-16 knots;<br>force 6 a <b>strong breeze</b>, 22-27 knots;<br>force 8 a <b>gale</b>, 34-40 knots.

![diagram](ds-beaufort.png)

## What are the wind speeds for Beaufort forces 5, 7 and 9?

Force 5 fresh breeze <b>17-21 kn</b>;<br>force 7 near gale <b>28-33 kn</b>;<br>force 9 <b>strong gale 41-47 kn</b> — which is the name on the Beaufort scale, though gale warnings and the shipping forecast call it <b>severe gale 9</b>.

![diagram](ds-beaufort.png)

## At what force do you expect to see 'many white horses' and think about the first reef?

Force 5, a fresh breeze of 17-21 knots: moderate waves, many white horses, some spray. Most cruising yachts want the first reef in at the top of force 4 or in force 5.

![diagram](ds-beaufort.png)

## What do the shipping forecast terms 'gale', 'severe gale', 'storm' and 'violent storm' mean?

<b>Gale</b> — mean wind force 8, or gusts of 43-51 kn.<br><b>Severe gale</b> — force 9, or gusts 52-60 kn.<br><b>Storm</b> — force 10, or gusts 61-68 kn.<br><b>Violent storm</b> — force 11, or gusts of 69 kn or more.

![diagram](ds-beaufort.png)

## What do 'imminent', 'soon' and 'later' mean in a shipping forecast gale warning?

<b>Imminent</b> — within 6 hours of the time of issue.<br><b>Soon</b> — 6 to 12 hours.<br><b>Later</b> — more than 12 hours.

![diagram](ds-beaufort.png)

## Define the marine visibility terms good, moderate, poor and very poor.

<b>Good</b> — more than 5 nautical miles.<br><b>Moderate</b> — 2 to 5 M.<br><b>Poor</b> — 1,000 m to 2 M.<br><b>Very poor</b> — less than 1,000 m.<br><b>Fog</b> is a term in the weather element rather than a visibility band, and fog means visibility below 1,000 m.

![diagram](ds-beaufort.png)

## Define the pressure-tendency terms used in the shipping forecast.

Change in the 3 hours before the observation, in hPa (1 hPa = 1 mb): <b>rising or falling slowly</b> 0.1-1.5;<br><b>rising or falling</b> 1.6-3.5;<br><b>rising or falling quickly</b> 3.6-6.0;<br><b>rising or falling very rapidly</b> more than 6.0. Also <b>rising or falling more slowly</b>, meaning the rate is easing, and <b>now rising</b> or <b>now falling</b>, meaning it has just turned. <b>Steady</b> means no appreciable change.

![diagram](ds-beaufort.png)

## What do 'backing' and 'veering' mean?

<b>Backing</b> — the wind direction changes <b>anticlockwise</b>, e.g. SW to S.<br><b>Veering</b> — it changes <b>clockwise</b>, e.g. SW to NW. In the northern hemisphere the wind veers as each front passes.

![diagram](ds-fronts.png)

## Which direction is a wind named for?

The direction it blows <b>from</b>. A westerly wind blows from the west. A tidal stream, by contrast, is named for the direction it flows <b>towards</b>.

## What are the parts of the shipping forecast, in order?

Gale warnings;<br>the general synopsis, meaning where the pressure systems are and where they are going;<br>then for each sea area in turn: <b>wind direction and force, sea state, weather, visibility</b>.<br>Then reports from coastal stations, and on some broadcasts the inshore waters forecast. It is issued four times a day and covers 24 hours.

## Where can you get a marine forecast at sea?

Met Office shipping and inshore waters forecasts on <b>BBC Radio 4 FM, DAB and BBC Sounds</b> — the 198 kHz long wave transmission closed on 27 June 2026;<br>Maritime Safety Information broadcasts by HM Coastguard on VHF (announced on Ch 16, given on a working channel);<br>NAVTEX on 518 kHz;<br>the Met Office and other websites and apps by mobile data;<br>harbour offices and marina noticeboards;<br>and barometer plus your own eyes.

## How does a depression behave in the northern hemisphere?

Pressure is lowest at the centre, wind circulates <b>anticlockwise</b> and blows slightly inwards across the isobars, and the system usually tracks east or north-east. Closely spaced isobars mean strong wind.

![diagram](ds-fronts.png)

## How does an anticyclone behave in the northern hemisphere?

High pressure with <b>clockwise</b> circulation and light winds, widely spaced isobars, settled weather. In summer it brings fine weather and sea breezes; in winter it can bring fog, low cloud and frost. Anticyclones move slowly and block depressions.

## State Buys Ballot's law.

In the northern hemisphere, if you stand with your back to the wind, low pressure is on your <b>left</b>. It lets you place the depression without a chart.

![diagram](ds-fronts.png)

## What is a warm front and what weather does it bring?

The boundary where warm air rides up over cold air. Ahead of it:<br>high cirrus thickening to altostratus and then low, thick cloud,<br>pressure falling steadily,<br>a long spell of continuous rain,<br>and the wind increasing — <b>backing as the depression first approaches, then veering through the frontal zone</b>. As the front passes the rain eases to drizzle, the wind <b>veers</b>, pressure steadies and the temperature rises.

![diagram](ds-fronts.png)

## What is a cold front and what weather does it bring?

Cold air undercutting warm air, giving a sharp, often squally transition:<br>towering cumulonimbus,<br>heavy showers or thunder,<br>a marked <b>veer</b> and gusting of the wind,<br>a sharp rise in pressure and a drop in temperature. Behind it the air is clear, bright and showery with good visibility.

![diagram](ds-fronts.png)

## What is the warm sector like?

The area between the warm and cold fronts:<br>overcast with low stratus,<br>drizzle,<br>mild and muggy,<br><b>poor visibility</b> and often sea fog,<br>with steady pressure and a steady wind direction.

![diagram](ds-fronts.png)

## What is an occlusion?

Where the cold front catches up with the warm front and lifts the warm sector clear of the surface. You get the cloud and rain of both fronts combined, but without the clear temperature change — a wet, murky passage of weather.

![diagram](ds-fronts.png)

## What is the classic sequence of wind and pressure as a depression passes to your north?

Starting from a south-westerly gradient wind: as the depression approaches from the west the wind <b>backs to SE</b> and freshens while pressure falls;<br>the warm front passes and the wind <b>veers to SW</b>, pressure steadies and visibility worsens;<br>the cold front passes and the wind <b>veers sharply to NW</b> and gusts, pressure rises and visibility clears. <b>At each front the wind veers.</b>

![diagram](ds-fronts.png)

## What is a trough and a ridge?

A <b>trough</b> is an elongated area of low pressure — expect a wind shift, cloud and showers even without a named front.<br>A <b>ridge</b> is an elongated area of high pressure, bringing a temporary improvement.

## What is a sea breeze, and when should you expect one?

On a sunny day the land heats faster than the sea, air rises over the land and cooler sea air flows in. It typically starts late morning,<br>blows onshore,<br>can reach force 4-5 by mid-afternoon and dies in the evening. It needs a sunny day and a light background wind, and it can reinforce or cancel the gradient wind.

## What is a land breeze?

The night-time reverse: the land cools faster than the sea, so a light offshore breeze flows from land to sea in the early hours. It is usually weaker than the sea breeze.

## What is advection fog (sea fog) and why does it matter to a yacht?

Warm, moist air moving over a colder sea surface is cooled below its dew point. It can form in a good breeze, persist for days, and it is the fog that catches yachts out in spring and early summer in the Channel — thickest often in the warm sector of a depression.

## What is radiation fog?

Fog formed overnight over land by radiative cooling under clear skies with light winds. It drifts out of estuaries and harbour entrances at dawn and usually burns off during the morning.

## How does the barometer help you if you have no forecast?

The trend matters more than the value. Falling steadily means a depression approaching: wind and rain. Falling <b>quickly</b>, 3.6-6.0 hPa in 3 hours, or <b>very rapidly</b>, more than 6.0 hPa in 3 hours, means a vigorous system and possibly gale-force wind. Rising means improvement, though a sharp rise behind a cold front comes with squalls.

## What clouds warn you of a squall or thunderstorm?

<b>Cumulonimbus</b> — tall, dark, anvil-topped. Expect a sudden violent wind shift and increase,<br>heavy rain or hail,<br>and a rapid drop in visibility. Reef before it arrives and get the crew clipped on.

## What is a katabatic wind and where might you meet one?

Cold dense air draining down a slope at night, producing a strong offshore gust that arrives without warning in a sheltered-looking anchorage under high ground — common in the Mediterranean, Norway and steep-sided lochs.

## How does the wind behave near a headland or a cliff?

It accelerates and bends round headlands, giving a stronger gust and a wind shift; under a cliff there may be a wind shadow, and then violent downdraughts and gusts a few boat lengths further out. Never assume the wind close inshore is the forecast wind.

## What does 'fog patches' or 'occasionally poor' mean in an inshore waters forecast?

It is a qualifier, not a promise: the poor visibility or fog is expected for <b>part of the period only, and not everywhere in the area</b>. The Met Office publishes no numerical definition of 'occasional' or 'patches'. Plan as if you will get it, and know where you can go if it closes in.

## What is the difference between the shipping forecast and the inshore waters forecast?

The shipping forecast covers the large sea areas offshore with mean winds and a 24-hour outlook. The <b>inshore waters</b> forecast covers up to 12 miles off the coast in more relevant detail — wind, weather, visibility, sea state — and is the one a Day Skipper should be using.

## What sea state terms are used, and what do they mean?

<b>Calm (glassy)</b> 0 m;<br><b>calm (rippled)</b> 0-0.1 m;<br><b>smooth</b> 0.1-0.5 m;<br><b>slight</b> 0.5-1.25 m;<br><b>moderate</b> 1.25-2.5 m;<br><b>rough</b> 2.5-4 m;<br><b>very rough</b> 4-6 m;<br><b>high</b> 6-9 m;<br><b>very high</b> 9-14 m;<br><b>phenomenal</b> more than 14 m. These are significant wave heights — individual waves can be substantially higher.

## How should the forecast affect your go / no-go decision?

Compare the forecast wind and sea with the limits of the <b>boat</b>, the <b>crew</b> (the least experienced person aboard) and the <b>passage</b>, including wind against tide, the exposure of the route and the state of the bar or entrance on arrival. If the answer is marginal for any of the three, do not go.

## What are Beaufort forces 0 to 3?

Force 0 <b>calm</b>, under 1 kn;<br>force 1 <b>light air</b>, 1-3 kn;<br>force 2 <b>light breeze</b>, 4-6 kn;<br>force 3 <b>gentle breeze</b>, 7-10 kn.

![diagram](ds-beaufort.png)

## What are Beaufort forces 10, 11 and 12?

Force 10 <b>storm</b>, 48-55 kn;<br>force 11 <b>violent storm</b>, 56-63 kn;<br>force 12 <b>hurricane force</b>, 64 kn or more.

![diagram](ds-beaufort.png)

## What does 'hurricane force' mean in a forecast, and why not just 'hurricane'?

Winds of force 12, 64 knots or more. The Met Office says <b>hurricane force</b> because <b>hurricane</b> on its own means a true tropical cyclone, which is not experienced in British waters.

## What is a strong wind warning, and why does it matter more to you than a gale warning?

A warning of winds of <b>force 6 or more</b> in inshore waters. It is issued well below gale force, and force 6 is the threshold at which most small craft should think again — so it is the warning a Day Skipper should act on.

## What do 'slowly', 'steadily', 'rather quickly', 'rapidly' and 'very rapidly' mean about a pressure system?

How fast the system is <b>moving</b>: slowly under 15 kn;<br>steadily 15-25 kn;<br>rather quickly 25-35 kn;<br>rapidly 35-45 kn;<br>very rapidly more than 45 kn. Do not confuse these with the pressure-tendency terms, which describe how fast the barometer is changing.

## What does 'becoming cyclonic' mean in a forecast?

There will be a <b>considerable change of wind direction across the path of a depression</b> within that area — a low is passing through, so expect the wind to swing right round rather than settle.

## What is in the coastal station reports, and what are they for?

Actual observations, not forecasts:<br><b>wind direction and force,<br>visibility,<br>sea-level pressure and pressure tendency</b>. Use them to check the forecast against reality, and to see where the system has actually got to.

## How much stronger than the mean wind are the gusts, and what should you plan for?

Typically 40 to 60% above the mean — one to two Beaufort forces. A forecast force 6 can gust force 8, which is why warnings quote gust bands. <b>Reef for the gusts, not the mean.</b>

![diagram](ds-beaufort.png)

## How can you anticipate sea fog before you sail?

Compare the forecast <b>dew point</b> with the <b>sea temperature</b>: if the air's dew point is at or above the sea temperature, warm moist air moving over the colder sea will fog. Classic on south-westerly winds in the Channel in spring and early summer — and it does not need a calm.
