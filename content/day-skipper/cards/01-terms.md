# 01 Boat and nautical terms

## Bow, stern, port, starboard — what does each mean?

<b>Bow</b> = the front.<br><b>Stern</b> = the back.<br><b>Port</b> = the left side facing forward (red).<br><b>Starboard</b> = the right side facing forward (green).

![diagram](fig:hull-plan@bow,stern,port,starboard)

## Amidships

The middle of the boat, both fore-and-aft and athwartships.

![diagram](fig:hull-plan@amidships,bow,stern)

## Beam (two meanings)

1\. The widest part of the hull, and so the boat's width.<br>2. A direction at right angles to the fore-and-aft line: 'abeam'.

![diagram](fig:hull-plan@beam)

## Draught

The vertical distance from the waterline to the lowest point of the keel — the minimum depth of water the boat needs.

![diagram](fig:hull-profile@draught,waterline)

## Air draught

The height from the waterline to the highest point of the boat, usually the masthead including the aerial. What matters for bridges and overhead cables.

![diagram](fig:hull-profile@air-draught,waterline)

## Freeboard

The vertical distance from the waterline to the deck edge.

![diagram](fig:hull-profile@freeboard,waterline)

## Windward and leeward

<b>Windward</b> is the side or direction the wind is coming from (also 'weather side'). <b>Leeward</b> ('loo-ard') is the side or direction the wind is going to — the sheltered side. On a sailing boat the leeward side is the boom's side.

![diagram](fig:windward-lee@wind,windward,leeward)

## Lee shore — and why it matters

A shore onto which the wind is blowing. It is dangerous: the wind and waves push you towards it, and it gives no shelter. A weather shore, with the wind blowing off it, is the sheltered one.

![diagram](fig:windward-lee@wind,lee-shore,weather-shore)

## Ahead, astern, abeam, abaft, forward of the beam

<b>Ahead</b> in front. <b>Astern</b> behind. <b>Abeam</b> at 90&deg; to the fore-and-aft line. <b>Forward of the beam</b> anywhere in the 90&deg; from ahead to abeam. <b>Abaft the beam</b> anywhere from abeam to astern.

![diagram](fig:bearings@ahead,astern,abeam,forward-beam,abaft-beam)

## Which direction is 22.5&deg; abaft the beam, and why does the number matter?

It is 112.5&deg; from ahead — the after cut-off of each sidelight and the boundary of the overtaking sector. Both sidelights together cover 225&deg;, matching the masthead light arc; the sternlight covers the remaining 135&deg;.

![diagram](fig:bearings@abaft-22,abeam,ahead)

## Heel versus list

<b>Heel</b> is a temporary lean caused by wind or crew weight. <b>List</b> is a permanent lean caused by unbalanced weight or flooding.

## The three rotational motions of a boat at sea

<b>Roll</b> — side to side about the fore-and-aft axis.<br><b>Pitch</b> — bow and stern rising and falling.<br><b>Yaw</b> — swinging left and right of the heading.

![diagram](fig:motions)

## Displacement

The weight of water the hull displaces, which equals the boat's total weight. A displacement hull is limited to a hull speed of roughly 1.34 &times; &radic;(waterline length in feet) in knots.

## What makes a cruising yacht stable?

The keel puts the centre of gravity low. When the boat heels, the centre of buoyancy moves outboard, so gravity and buoyancy form a <b>righting moment</b> that pushes her upright. Beyond the angle of vanishing stability that moment reverses and she will capsize.

## Reserve buoyancy

The watertight volume above the waterline. It provides the extra buoyancy that lifts the boat over waves and resists swamping — one reason for closing hatches and washboards at sea.

## What happens to stability if you fill the water tanks and load the anchor locker?

Weight low down (tanks, keel, batteries) improves stability. Weight high up or far forward (anchor and chain in the bow, gear on deck, crew on the coachroof) raises the centre of gravity, reduces stability and makes the boat pitch harder. Free surface in a part-full tank also reduces stability.

## Name the three sides and three corners of a mainsail.

Sides: <b>luff</b> (leading edge), <b>leech</b> (trailing edge), <b>foot</b> (bottom).<br>Corners: <b>head</b> (top), <b>tack</b> (bottom forward), <b>clew</b> (bottom aft).

![diagram](fig:sail-parts@head,tack,clew,luff,leech,foot)

## Standing rigging versus running rigging

<b>Standing rigging</b> is fixed and holds the mast up: forestay,<br>backstay,<br>shrouds,<br>spreaders,<br>chainplates.<br><br><b>Running rigging</b> moves and controls the sails: halyards,<br>sheets,<br>reefing lines,<br>kicker,<br>topping lift,<br>cunningham,<br>outhaul.

![diagram](fig:rigging)

## Halyard versus sheet

A <b>halyard</b> hoists a sail (pulls it up). A <b>sheet</b> trims a sail (pulls it in or lets it out).

![diagram](fig:rigging@halyard,sheet)

## Kicker (boom vang) — what does it do?

It pulls the boom down, controlling leech tension and stopping the boom lifting when the sail is eased. Without it the mainsail twists open and loses drive off the wind.

![diagram](fig:rigging@kicker)

## Topping lift

A line from the masthead to the end of the boom that holds the boom up when the mainsail is lowered or reefed.

![diagram](fig:rigging@topping-lift)

## Genoa versus jib

Both are headsails. A <b>jib</b>'s clew does not reach past the mast; a <b>genoa</b> overlaps the mast and is larger. A furling headsail can be partly rolled to reduce area.

## What is a seacock and why must you know where they all are?

A valve on a hull fitting below or near the waterline (engine cooling intake, heads, sinks, log). They are your first defence against flooding, so every crew member should know their location and how to shut them, and softwood bungs should be stowed nearby.

## Bilge, sole, bulkhead

<b>Bilge</b> — the lowest internal space where water collects.<br><b>Sole</b> — the cabin or cockpit floor.<br><b>Bulkhead</b> — a transverse structural partition.

## Pulpit, pushpit, stanchions, guardrails, jackstay

<b>Pulpit</b> — the rail at the bow.<br><b>Pushpit</b> — the rail at the stern.<br><b>Stanchions</b> — the posts supporting the <b>guardrails</b> along the deck.<br>A <b>jackstay</b> is a wire or webbing line running fore and aft that you clip a harness tether to.

![diagram](fig:deck-fittings@pulpit,pushpit,stanchions,guardrails,jackstay)

## Warp, spring, breast line

<b>Warps</b> are mooring lines: bow line and stern line run forward and aft. <b>Springs</b> run at a shallow angle to stop fore-and-aft movement. <b>Breast lines</b> run at right angles to hold the boat in to the pontoon.

![diagram](fig:mooring-lines@bow-line,stern-line,fore-spring,back-spring,breast-line)

## What is a transit?

Two identifiable objects seen in line. It gives a position line with no compass error at all, and shows instantly whether you are being pushed off it — the single most reliable pilotage tool.

## Cable (as a unit)

One tenth of a nautical mile, so about 185 metres. Also the collective word for the anchor chain or warp.

## Fathom

Six feet, about 1.83 m. Old charts are in fathoms and feet; modern metric charts are in metres. Always check the chart's depth units.

## Fenders, fairleads, cleats and bollards

A <b>fender</b> cushions the hull. A <b>fairlead</b> guides a line and takes the chafe. A <b>cleat</b> or <b>bollard</b> is what you make the line fast to. Never lead a warp over a sharp edge.

![diagram](fig:deck-fittings@cleat,fairlead)

## Keel types and what each means for you

<b>Long keel</b> — tracks well, turns slowly, sits upright when dried out.<br><b>Fin keel</b> — fast and manoeuvrable, less directional stability, needs support to dry out.<br><b>Bilge keels</b> — shallower draught, will dry out level, slower to windward.<br><b>Lifting keel or centreboard</b> — variable draught.<br><b>Wing keel</b> — shallower with a bulb for stability.

## What does 'make fast', 'belay', 'surge' and 'snub' mean?

<b>Make fast</b> / <b>belay</b> — secure a line.<br><b>Surge</b> — let a line slip in a controlled way round a winch or cleat.<br><b>Snub</b> — check a running line sharply to take the load.

## Riding turn

A turn of rope trapped over itself on a winch drum so it will not release. Take the load off with a rolling hitch onto a spare line, then clear it.

## Chafe — why is it the thing that actually breaks mooring lines?

A rope under load moving over any edge wears through from the outside, often overnight and unseen. Use fairleads, chafe sleeves or a length of hose, and inspect lines where they pass over the toe rail.
