# 19 Emergencies

## What are the immediate actions for a man overboard?

Shout 'man overboard' to get everyone on deck;<br><b>throw</b> buoyancy — danbuoy and lifebuoy — immediately;<br>detail one person to <b>point</b> at the casualty and do nothing else;<br>press the MOB button on the plotter or GNSS;<br>start the engine, checking for lines over the side;<br>and manoeuvre back to the casualty.

![diagram](ds-mob.png)

## Describe the reach-tack-reach MOB recovery under sail.

Get onto a <b>beam reach</b> — bearing away if you were beating, luffing up if you were running — and sail on for four to six boat lengths.<br><b>Tack</b>, never gybe.<br>Come onto the reciprocal beam reach, bear away enough to get to leeward of the casualty, then turn up and approach on a <b>close reach</b>, controlling speed by easing the sails.<br>Stop with the casualty alongside on the <b>leeward</b> side at or just forward of the shrouds, engine out of gear — never ahead of the bow, where you lose sight of them.

![diagram](ds-mob.png)

## Describe the crash-stop (heave-to) MOB recovery.

Tack immediately without touching the headsail sheet, so the headsail is left backed. The boat stops quickly and stays near the casualty. Then drop or furl the headsail and motor or sail the last few lengths. Its virtue is that you never get far from the casualty. Note that the American <b>Quick Stop</b> is a different manoeuvre: you keep turning through a gybe and circle back.

![diagram](ds-mob.png)

## Why do you approach a man overboard from downwind, and stop with them to leeward?

Approaching from downwind on a close reach lets you spill the sails to control speed and stop. Recover on the <b>leeward</b> side because a yacht blows downwind far faster than a person in the water, so a casualty left to windward is one you drift away from; the hull also gives them a lee, and the heeled leeward rail is the lowest freeboard. Stop the boat and take the engine out of gear before they are anywhere near the hull.

![diagram](ds-mob.png)

## Why should the engine be out of gear before anyone comes alongside the hull?

Because the propeller will kill or maim someone in the water. Neutral, and preferably stopped, before the casualty is anywhere near the stern or the boarding ladder.

![diagram](ds-mob.png)

## Why lift a MOB casualty horizontally if you can?

Because prolonged immersion causes the blood to pool in the legs. Lifting vertically can drop the blood pressure and cause post-immersion collapse and cardiac arrest. Keep them horizontal during and after recovery, and handle them gently.

## How can you get a casualty back on board a yacht?

A boarding ladder if they can climb;<br>a lifting strop or MOB recovery sling on a spare halyard led to a winch;<br>the mainsail or a sail used as a scoop under them;<br>a bight of rope as a step;<br>or the dinghy. Practise it — a wet, cold adult in oilskins is an extraordinarily heavy lift.

## When would you send a MAYDAY for a man overboard?

As soon as you lose sight of the casualty, or if you are short-handed, or if the first recovery attempt fails, or if there is any injury. It is far better to cancel a MAYDAY than to send it too late.

![diagram](ds-vhf-distress.png)

## What is a MAYDAY and how do you send one?

The distress signal for grave and imminent danger. Press and hold the <b>DSC distress</b> button for 5 seconds first, then on Ch 16:<br>MAYDAY &times;3,<br>'THIS IS' + vessel name &times;3,<br>MMSI and callsign,<br>then MAYDAY + name + MMSI,<br>position,<br>nature of distress,<br>assistance required,<br>number of people on board,<br>any other useful information,<br>OVER.

![diagram](ds-vhf-distress.png)

## What is a PAN-PAN used for?

Urgency: a very urgent message about the safety of the vessel or a person, where there is <b>not</b> grave and imminent danger —<br>engine failure in a safe position,<br>a broken bone,<br>a dismasting with no injury,<br>or a request for medical advice.

![diagram](ds-vhf-distress.png)

## What is a SÉCURITÉ message?

A safety message: a navigational or meteorological warning. The Coastguard uses it to broadcast gale warnings and hazards; you would use it to report a container adrift or an unlit obstruction.

![diagram](ds-vhf-distress.png)

## What is a MAYDAY RELAY?

A distress message passed on behalf of another vessel that cannot transmit its own, or whose distress you have witnessed. You broadcast 'MAYDAY RELAY' three times, then 'THIS IS' your own vessel, and pass on the details.

![diagram](ds-vhf-distress.png)

## What are SEELONCE MAYDAY and SEELONCE FEENEE?

<b>SEELONCE MAYDAY</b> imposes radio silence on Ch 16 so distress traffic can be heard; it is normally sent by the station controlling the distress. <b>SEELONCE FEENEE</b> ends the silence and returns the channel to normal working.

![diagram](ds-vhf-distress.png)

## What is Ch 16 for, and what is Ch 70?

<b>Ch 16</b> is the distress, safety and calling channel, monitored by the Coastguard — keep a listening watch on it. <b>Ch 70</b> carries <b>DSC data only</b>: never transmit voice on it.

![diagram](ds-vhf-distress.png)

## What is DSC and what does a distress alert send?

Digital Selective Calling: a data burst on Ch 70 containing your MMSI number, the nature of distress if you have time to select it, and your GNSS position and time. It is received automatically by the Coastguard and by DSC-equipped vessels nearby. Always follow it with a voice MAYDAY on Ch 16.

![diagram](ds-vhf-distress.png)

## What qualification do you need to operate a marine VHF, and what is an MMSI?

The <b>Short Range Certificate</b> (SRC), and the vessel needs a Ship Radio Licence. The <b>MMSI</b> is the nine-digit Maritime Mobile Service Identity that identifies your station in DSC calls — it must be programmed correctly and registered.

## List the recognised distress signals.

Continuous sounding of a fog-signal apparatus;<br>a gun or explosive signal at about 1-minute intervals;<br>rockets or shells throwing red stars fired one at a time at short intervals;<br>red parachute or hand flare;<br>orange smoke;<br>SOS by any means;<br>the spoken word 'MAYDAY';<br>code flags <b>NC</b>;<br>a square flag with a ball above or below;<br>flames on the vessel;<br>slowly and repeatedly raising and lowering outstretched arms;<br>a DSC alert;<br>EPIRB, PLB or SART.

![diagram](ds-vhf-distress.png)

## What do you do if you are taking on water?

Get the crew into lifejackets and send a distress or urgency call early.<br>Find the source — seacocks, hoses, stern gland, log impeller, keel bolts, a holed hull — and stem it with a bung, a cushion, a sail or anything you can jam and brace in place.<br>Run all the pumps and bail.<br>Head for shallow water or a beach if you cannot control it.

## What do you do if the steering fails?

Take the way off.<br>Rig the <b>emergency tiller</b> on the rudder stock — know where it lives before you need it.<br>If the rudder itself is gone, balance the sails to steer, or stream a drogue or bucket on a bridle from the quarters and adjust the lines to steer.<br>If you cannot manoeuvre, <b>exhibit</b> two all-round red lights in a vertical line, or two black balls by day, for not under command, and sound one prolonged plus two short blasts in restricted visibility.

## You run aground. What do you check first, and what changes depending on whether the tide is rising or falling?

Check the crew, then check for damage and water ingress.<br>Stop the engine if you may have fouled something or may suck in sand.<br>Sound round the boat with a lead line or from the dinghy to find which way the deep water lies — it is often not the way you came.<br>Note whether the tide is rising or falling, because that decides everything.<br>On a <b>rising</b> tide, and on a fin keel, heel her with crew weight or the boom out to reduce draught and motor or kedge off towards the deep water; heeling a bilge-keeler or long-keeler gains you little.<br>On a <b>falling</b> tide act within seconds or prepare to sit it out: lay a kedge to hold her, pad and support the side she will lie on, close the seacocks and hatches, and get the crew somewhere safe.

## What do you do if you are dismasted?

Secure the crew first.<br>Stop the engine to keep rigging out of the propeller.<br>Get the rig alongside and lash it, or cut it away with bolt croppers if it is holing the hull.<br>Recover what you can for a jury rig, motor if you can, and make a PAN-PAN or MAYDAY according to the danger.

## How do you take a tow?

Agree the plan on VHF first.<br>Use your strongest points — round a <b>keel-stepped</b> mast, never a deck-stepped one, or through both bow fairleads to the primary winches or a substantial cleat so the load is spread — with chafe protection.<br>Keep the tow long and elastic, go slowly, and have a crew member ready with a knife at the towline.<br>Nobody stands in line with a loaded rope.

## What do you need to know about a helicopter rescue?

Follow the winchman's instructions exactly and only over VHF Ch 16 or as directed.<br>Lower sails if told to, keep the deck clear, secure loose gear, brief the crew, do not touch the wire until it has earthed on the deck or in the water, and never make it fast to the boat.<br>Motor a steady course as directed, usually with the wind on a bow.

## What is the first thing you should do in almost any emergency?

Stop, take a breath and account for your crew. Then decide whether you need outside help and call for it <b>early</b> — the situation will not get simpler, and rescue assets need time to reach you.

## What is the procedure for cancelling a false or resolved distress alert?

Do not just switch off. Transmit on Ch 16:<br>'All stations' &times;3,<br>'this is' + vessel name,<br>MMSI and callsign,<br>the time,<br>'cancel my distress alert of [time]' and 'out'.<br>Tell the Coastguard what happened. An uncancelled alert sends people to sea for nothing.

## How do you recover a man overboard under power?

Same immediate actions — shout, throw, point, MOB button.<br>Then turn <b>towards</b> the side the casualty went over, so the stern and the propeller swing away from them.<br>Approach slowly into the wind and sea so the boat lies steady, aim to stop with the casualty on the <b>leeward</b> side at the shrouds, and put the engine in <b>neutral</b>, preferably stopped, before anyone is near the hull.<br>Never approach with the casualty ahead of the bow, where you lose sight of them.

![diagram](ds-mob.png)

## You have lost sight of the man overboard. What now?

<b>MAYDAY immediately</b> — do not search first and call later.<br>Note the MOB waypoint and the time, and work out the datum: the casualty drifts with the tidal stream plus a little leeway, and the boat drifts faster.<br>Return down the reciprocal of your track at slow speed with every crew member searching an allocated sector, and search <b>across</b> the tide.<br>Keep passing the position and elapsed time to the Coastguard so they can compute their own drift.

![diagram](ds-mob.png)
