# 06 Lights and shapes

## Define masthead light, sidelights, sternlight and all-round light with their arcs.

<b>Masthead light</b> — white, 225&deg;, from ahead to 22.5&deg; abaft the beam on each side, on the fore-and-aft centreline.<br><b>Sidelights</b> — green to starboard and red to port, each 112.5&deg;, from ahead to 22.5&deg; abaft the beam.<br><b>Sternlight</b> — white, 135&deg;, 67.5&deg; each side of right aft.<br><b>All-round light</b> — 360&deg;. In a vessel under 20 m the sidelights may be combined in <b>one lantern on the centreline</b>. A <b>towing light</b> is yellow with the sternlight's 135&deg; characteristics, and a <b>flashing light</b> flashes 120 times a minute or more.

## What lights does a power-driven vessel under 50 m show when underway?

A masthead light, sidelights and a sternlight. Under 12 m she may instead show an <b>all-round white light and sidelights</b>. Under 7 m, and not exceeding 7 knots, she may show an <b>all-round white light alone</b>, with sidelights only if practicable.

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## What extra light does a power-driven vessel of 50 m or more show?

A <b>second masthead light abaft of and higher than</b> the forward one. Seeing two white masthead lights in a vertical line tells you it is a big ship, and their relative position gives you her aspect.

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## What lights does a sailing vessel under sail show?

Sidelights and a sternlight — and <b>no masthead light</b>. Under 20 m these may be combined in a single tricolour lantern at or near the top of the mast. She may instead add an optional all-round <b>red over green</b> at or near the top of the mast — but <b>never together with the tricolour</b> (Rule 25(c)).

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## Why must you not use a masthead tricolour and deck-level sidelights at the same time?

Because you would then show two sets of sidelights, which is not a recognised light pattern and confuses another vessel about your size and aspect. Use one or the other.

## What lights may a sailing vessel under 7 m, or a vessel under oars, show?

A sailing vessel under 7 m shows the normal lights, or a tricolour, <b>if practicable</b>; if she does not, she must have ready at hand an <b>electric torch or lighted lantern showing a white light</b> to exhibit in sufficient time to prevent collision. A vessel under oars has the same torch option at <b>any</b> length (Rule 25(d)).

## What lights does a vessel at anchor show?

An all-round white light forward where it can best be seen; a vessel of 50 m or more shows a second one aft and lower. Any vessel at anchor <b>may</b>, and one of <b>100 m or more shall</b>, use her working lights to illuminate her decks. By day, one <b>black ball</b>. A vessel under 7 m is exempt unless anchored in or near a narrow channel, fairway, anchorage or where other vessels normally navigate.

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## What lights and shapes does a vessel aground show?

The anchor light(s) plus <b>two all-round red lights</b> in a vertical line. By day, <b>three black balls</b> in a vertical line. A vessel under 12 m aground is not required to show these (Rule 30(f)) — she still shows her anchor light.

## What lights and shapes does a vessel not under command show?

<b>Two all-round red lights</b> in a vertical line, plus sidelights and sternlight if making way. By day, <b>two black balls</b>. A vessel under 12 m is not required to show these (Rule 27(g)), but doing so, or attracting attention under Rule 36, is still the seamanlike thing. Mnemonic: 'red over red, the captain is dead'.

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## What lights and shapes does a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre show?

<b>Red, white, red</b> all-round lights in a vertical line, plus normal steaming lights if making way. By day, <b>ball, diamond, ball</b> in a vertical line. A vessel under 12 m is not required to show these, except when engaged in diving operations (Rule 27(g)).

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## What lights and shape does a vessel constrained by her draught show?

She <b>may</b> show, in addition to her normal power-driven lights, <b>three all-round red lights</b> in a vertical line; by day, a black <b>cylinder</b>. Because it is optional, a deep-draught ship in a dredged channel may show nothing special — judge by the channel, not by the lights.

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## What lights and shape does a trawler show?

<b>Green over white</b> all-round lights, plus sidelights and sternlight when making way. By day, two cones apexes together in a vertical line; under 20 m she may show a <b>basket</b> instead. A trawler of 50 m or more also shows a masthead light abaft of and higher than the green. Mnemonic: 'green over white, trawling tonight'.

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## What lights does a vessel fishing other than trawling show?

<b>Red over white</b> all-round lights, plus sidelights and sternlight when making way. If the gear extends more than 150 m horizontally, an all-round white light — or by day a cone <b>apex upwards</b> — in the direction of the gear. By day, two cones apexes together, or a <b>basket</b> if under 20 m. Mnemonic: 'red over white, fishing at night'.

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## What lights does a vessel towing show, and what changes once the tow exceeds 200 m?

<b>Two masthead lights</b> in a vertical line,<br>sidelights,<br>sternlight,<br>and a <b>yellow towing light</b> above the sternlight. If the tow exceeds 200 m she shows three masthead lights in a vertical line and, by day, a black <b>diamond</b> on both towing and towed vessel.

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## What lights does a pilot vessel on duty show?

<b>White over red</b> all-round lights at or near the masthead, plus sidelights and sternlight when underway, or anchor lights when at anchor. Mnemonic: 'white over red, pilot ahead'.

![diagram](ds-lights-recognition.png)

## What lights and shape does a vessel engaged in dredging or underwater operations show?

The restricted-in-ability-to-manoeuvre lights (<b>red, white, red</b>) or shapes (<b>ball, diamond, ball</b>), plus masthead light, sidelights and sternlight when making way; and where an obstruction exists, <b>two all-round red lights or two balls</b> on the obstructed side and <b>two all-round green lights or two diamonds</b> on the side it is safe to pass. At anchor she shows these <b>instead of</b> the Rule 30 anchor lights — so pass on the green side.

## What lights does a vessel engaged in minesweeping show?

In addition to her power-driven lights, or her anchor lights if at anchor, <b>three all-round green lights or three balls</b> — one near the foremast head and one at each end of the fore yard. They mean it is dangerous to approach within <b>1,000 m</b>.

## What lights does a hovercraft in non-displacement mode show?

Her normal power-driven vessel lights plus an all-round <b>flashing yellow</b> light.

## You see a red light and a green light and a white light above and between them, all steady. What is it and what do you do?

A power-driven vessel of under 50 m, coming towards you more or less head-on. You are seeing both sidelights so she is roughly bow-on. If you are also power-driven this is a Rule 14 head-on situation — both alter to starboard. Keep watching the bearing.

## You see only a green light and a white masthead light. What are you looking at?

The starboard side of a power-driven vessel crossing from your left to your right. She is on your port side, so if you are power-driven you are the stand-on vessel — but keep checking the bearing.

## You see only a red light and a white masthead light. What are you looking at, and what must you do?

The port side of a power-driven vessel crossing from your right to your left. She is on <b>your starboard side</b>, so if you are power-driven you must <b>give way</b>: alter to starboard and pass astern of her.

## You see a single white light and nothing else. Name four things it could be.

The sternlight of a vessel you are overtaking;<br>the anchor light of a vessel at anchor;<br>the all-round white light of a small power-driven vessel under 12 m seen from astern;<br>a vessel under 7 m showing a torch;<br>one of the two masthead lights of a big ship at extreme range;<br>or the all-round white light of a power-driven vessel under 7 m doing 7 knots or less, which may legally show <b>no sidelights at all</b> and looks the same from every direction. Treat it with suspicion and take a bearing.

## You see two white lights in a vertical line, the higher one to the right of the lower. What is it doing?

The higher of two masthead lights is the <b>after</b> one, so her stern is to the right and her bow to the left: a power-driven vessel of 50 m or more crossing right to left. Confirm with the sidelight. <b>If you can see no sidelight at all, suspect a large vessel at anchor</b> — her anchor lights are also two whites at different heights, but the <b>forward</b> one is higher, and she is not moving.

## Sector lights — what are they for?

A light that shows different colours over different arcs, so its colour tells you which sector you are in. Typically <b>white</b> over the safe approach and <b>red</b> and <b>green</b> over the dangerous sides. Charted as, for example, Oc WRG 10s, with the sector bearings given <b>from seaward towards the light</b>.

## Leading lights

Two lights, a front and a rear, at different heights. Keep them vertically in line and you are on the leading line into the harbour. The chart gives the leading line's bearing.

## A directional light

Shows a narrow white beam along the safe approach with red and green either side, giving a precise line to steer without a second mark.

## What is the difference between the range of a light and its visibility to you?

The charted range is usually the <b>nominal range</b> in clear weather at a stated luminosity, or the <b>luminous range</b>. What you actually see is also limited by the <b>geographical range</b>, which depends on the height of the light and your own height of eye and so on the curvature of the earth. In poor visibility you may see much less than either.

## What is 'loom' and 'rising' or 'dipping' a light?

The <b>loom</b> is the glow of a light seen before the light itself, reflected off haze. <b>Raising</b> or <b>dipping</b> a light is the moment it appears or disappears over the horizon; with the light's height and your height of eye you can turn that into a distance off, which gives a position line.

## How far must navigation lights be visible, by vessel length?

Minimum ranges under Rule 22. <b>50 m or more</b>: masthead 6 M; sidelights, sternlight, towing and all-round lights 3 M. <b>12 m up to 50 m</b>: masthead 5 M, or 3 M if under 20 m; all others 2 M. <b>Under 12 m</b>: masthead 2 M, sidelights 1 M, sternlight, towing and all-round 2 M. Your sidelights may be visible only a mile — assume a ship has not seen you.

## What lights must a yacht show at night when motor-sailing?

The lights of a <b>power-driven vessel</b>: masthead (steaming) light, sidelights and sternlight. You <b>cannot</b> use a masthead tricolour, because there is no masthead light in it and steaming light plus tricolour is not a recognised pattern — switch to deck-level sidelights and sternlight and put the steaming light on. By day, the black cone point down.

## What does a small vessel engaged in diving operations show?

Where she is too small to show the full dredging signals: <b>red, white, red</b> all-round lights in a vertical line, and a <b>rigid replica of code flag A</b> not less than 1 m high, visible all round (Rule 27(e)). Divers are in the water, so pass wide and slow. The under-12 m exemption in Rule 27(g) does <b>not</b> apply to diving.

## Which lights and shapes is a small vessel excused from showing?

<b>Under 12 m</b> — need not show the NUC or RAM lights of Rule 27, except when diving; need not show the aground lights or shapes; need not carry a whistle or bell.<br><b>Under 7 m</b> — at anchor, no light or ball needed unless in or near a narrow channel, fairway, anchorage or where other vessels normally navigate; under sail, a torch or lantern in lieu if the normal lights are impracticable. Being excused is not the same as being invisible: show something.
