# 03 Anchoring

## How do you choose an anchorage?

Shelter from the wind now <b>and</b> after the forecast change;<br>good holding ground;<br>enough depth at low water and swinging room at all states of tide;<br>out of the fairway, cables and prohibited areas;<br>moderate tidal stream;<br>and an easy exit if the weather turns. A lee shore is not shelter.

## How much scope do you let out?

As a working rule, <b>4 times the maximum depth for all chain</b> and <b>6 times for rope with a chain leader</b>. Increase it in strong wind, swell or poor holding. Calculate from the depth at high water, not the depth when you arrive.

![diagram](ds-anchoring.png)

## What depth do you use to work out scope?

The <b>maximum</b> depth you will see while anchored — charted depth plus the height of tide at the <b>highest high water during your whole stay</b>, plus the height of the bow roller above the water. Then check that the <b>minimum</b> depth, at the lowest low water, still clears your keel.

## Good and bad holding ground

Good: firm sand, mud and clay.<br>Poor: rock, shingle, and especially weed, which stops the anchor reaching the bottom.<br>The chart's seabed abbreviations tell you: S sand,<br>M mud,<br>Cy clay,<br>R rock,<br>Wd weed,<br>Sh shells,<br>G gravel,<br>St stones.

## Describe the anchoring drill.

Brief the crew and prepare the anchor and cable.<br>Motor slowly up-wind or up-tide to the chosen spot and stop over the ground.<br>Lower the anchor as the boat starts to make way astern, paying out cable steadily so it lays in a line.<br>Snub the cable, then go gently astern to dig the anchor in.<br>Check the holding on transits or bearings, log the position and depth, and show the correct signal.

## Why lower the anchor rather than throw it, and why with the boat moving astern?

Throwing it piles the chain on top of the anchor and fouls it. Making way astern lays the cable out in a straight line so the anchor gets a horizontal pull and digs in instead of skipping.

## How do you check the anchor is holding?

Take two transits ashore, or a couple of bearings, and watch them: if they stay put you are holding. Feel the cable — a dragging anchor sends a juddering vibration up it. Set a GNSS anchor alarm as a back-up, never as the only check.

## Signals for a vessel at anchor

By day, one <b>black ball</b> forward.<br>By night, an <b>all-round white light</b> where it can best be seen; a vessel of 50 m or more shows two, the forward one higher. Vessels under 7 m are exempt unless anchored in or near a channel, fairway, anchorage or where other vessels normally navigate.

## Sound signal for a vessel at anchor in restricted visibility

Rapid ringing of the bell for about 5 seconds at intervals of not more than one minute. A vessel of 100 m or more also sounds a gong aft. She may in addition sound one short, one prolonged and one short blast to warn an approaching vessel.

## How do you weigh anchor?

Motor slowly up towards the anchor as the crew recovers the cable, so the engine does the work, not the windlass or the crew. When the cable is up and down, break the anchor out,<br>wash off the mud,<br>and stow and secure it before you leave.

## The anchor is fouled and will not come up — what can you do?

Take a turn of the cable on a cleat and use the boat's buoyancy in a swell, or motor slowly over it from a different direction to break it out. An anchor buoy and tripping line rigged to the crown before you anchor is the real answer in a known foul anchorage.

## CQR or plough anchor

A hinged plough. A good all-rounder that holds well in mud and sand, but it can be slow to set and needs weight to bury.

## Danforth or Fortress anchor

Twin flat flukes. Excellent holding in sand and mud for its weight and it stows flat, but it performs poorly in weed and can trip and skip if the pull changes direction.

## Bruce or claw anchor

Sets very readily and resets well after a wind shift, but has lower holding power for its weight and is awkward to stow.

## Modern scoop anchors (Rocna, Spade, Vulcan, Excel)

Concave-fluke, weighted-tip or roll-bar designs that set fast and give very high holding power for their weight. Now the common choice for cruising yachts. The <b>Delta</b> is <b>not</b> one of these: it is a fixed-shank, convex-fluke plough, a good all-rounder that sets more reliably than a CQR.

## Fisherman anchor

Traditional stock-and-fluke design. The one anchor that reliably hooks into rock and heavy weed, but it has low holding in sand and mud and is awkward to stow.

## Why is chain better than rope for anchoring?

Its weight makes the cable hang in a curve (catenary) so the pull on the anchor stays horizontal and shock loads are absorbed, and it resists chafe on the seabed. Rope is lighter and cheaper but needs more scope and a chain leader at the anchor end.

## What is a swinging circle and how big is it?

The circle the boat can sweep as the wind or tide turns her: radius = the scope let out plus the length of the boat. Check it clears other boats, moorings and shallow water at all states of tide, and remember boats on chain and on rope swing differently.

## Anchoring in a strong tidal stream with a wind against it

Boats will lie to the stronger of the two, and may sheer about. Allow more scope,<br>more swinging room,<br>keep a watch,<br>and expect the boat to lie across the wind. Consider a second anchor laid astern, or move to a better berth.

## Why keep an anchor watch?

Because an anchor that has held for six hours can break out the moment the tide turns or the wind rises. Watch transits and depth, keep the engine ready, and know your escape route in the dark before you need it.

## What is a kedge anchor for?

A second, lighter anchor. Use it to lay out astern to stop the boat swinging,<br>to hold her off after grounding,<br>to haul off a lee shore,<br>or as a spare if the main anchor is lost or fouled. It is usually laid from the dinghy, or carried out and dropped clear.
