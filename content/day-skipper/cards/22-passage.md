# 22 Passage planning and victualling

## What does SOLAS Chapter V require of a small craft skipper?

Regulation 34 requires you to plan the passage before you go:<br>weather,<br>tides,<br>limitations of the vessel,<br>the crew,<br>navigational dangers,<br>a contingency plan,<br>and leaving details of your intentions with someone ashore. Chapter V also requires you to carry a <b>radar reflector</b> if practicable,<br>to know the <b>lifesaving signals</b>,<br>to <b>assist anyone in distress</b> at sea,<br>to <b>report dangers to navigation</b>,<br>and not to misuse distress signals.

## List the elements of a passage plan.

Weather forecast and how you will update it;<br>tidal heights and streams and any tidal gates;<br>limitations of the vessel (draught, air draught, fuel and water range, gear);<br>the crew's experience, fitness and hours of darkness;<br>navigational dangers;<br>a contingency plan with bolt holes;<br>and information ashore.

## What is a bolt hole and why does every leg need one?

A port or anchorage you can divert to if the weather, the crew or the boat changes the plan. Identify it in advance with its approach, its tidal restrictions and the conditions in which it is usable, because you will be choosing it when you are tired and it is dark.

## What do you leave with someone ashore?

Your destination and route,<br>ETA,<br>the boat's name, description and sail number,<br>the number of people aboard,<br>your contact details and safety equipment,<br>and clear instructions on what to do — and what <b>not</b> to do — if you are overdue. Then tell them when you arrive.

## How do you work out an ETA for a passage leg?

Distance divided by expected speed over the ground for each leg, allowing for the tide hour by hour and for a realistic boat speed rather than your best ever. Add contingency time, and check the arrival time against the tidal window at the destination.

## How do you plan a passage around tidal gates?

Work backwards from the gate. Decide the time you must be at each gate (a headland at slack water,<br>a bar on a rising tide,<br>a lock or sill within its window), then compute the departure time from the passage times between them. If the gates do not line up, the passage does not work — change the day.

## What are the limitations of the vessel that should shape a plan?

Draught and air draught,<br>fuel and water capacity and range under power,<br>the sail wardrobe and reefing arrangements,<br>the state of the gear,<br>whether you have self-steering,<br>the navigation and communications kit,<br>how she behaves in a head sea,<br>and whether she can be sailed short-handed if someone is out of action.

## How do you plan a watch system for a short-handed crew on a night passage?

Keep watches short enough to stay alert — typically 2 to 3 hours at night — with a clear handover briefing covering position,<br>course,<br>traffic,<br>weather and any changes. Make sure everyone gets sleep before as well as during, and that the skipper is not the only person who can navigate.

## What should you brief the crew on before a passage?

The plan and the ETA;<br>the weather;<br>who does what and the watch system;<br>lifejackets, harnesses and clipping points;<br>MOB drill;<br>how to send a distress call and where the position is displayed;<br>seacocks, bilge pumps, gas, heads and galley;<br>fire extinguishers and the liferaft;<br>and the rule that anyone can raise a concern at any time.

## What is victualling, and what does a Day Skipper need to think about?

Provisioning for the passage: enough food and, above all, <b>water</b> for the planned trip plus a margin for delay;<br>food that can be prepared and eaten in a seaway (one-pot meals, sandwiches, soup in a flask made before you leave);<br>hot drinks;<br>snacks in the cockpit;<br>and allowance for seasickness reducing appetite while dehydration rises.

## Why make food and hot drinks before you leave harbour?

Because cooking in a lumpy sea is difficult, dangerous and the fastest route to seasickness. A flask of soup or stew prepared alongside keeps a cold night watch functioning.

## What documents should be aboard for a cross-Channel passage?

Passports for everyone,<br>the ship's registration and Ship Radio Licence,<br>evidence of VAT status and insurance,<br>the skipper's certificate of competence and SRC,<br>a crew list,<br>and — for France and some other countries — an ICC or national equivalent. Check the current entry requirements before you go.

## Arriving in the UK from abroad: when does the Q flag go up, and how do you report?

Arriving in the UK from outside the UK — the Channel Islands included — you must fly the plain yellow <b>Q</b> flag as soon as you enter UK waters, the 12-mile limit, and keep it up until reporting is complete; failing to do so is a penalty offence. Report through Border Force's <b>sPCR</b> online service, or the C1331 fallback; outbound voyages are notified 2 to 24 hours before departure. Arrivals into Northern Ireland from an EU state need no Q flag. These rules change — check the current gov.uk guidance for both countries before you sail.

## How do you decide on the day whether to go?

Compare the actual forecast with the limits of boat, crew and passage;<br>check the tidal windows still work;<br>look at wind against tide and at the state of the entrance you are leaving and the one you are arriving at;<br>and consider what happens if the wind is 10 knots more than forecast. The decision to stay in port is a seamanlike decision, not a failure.

## What do you write in the deck log, and why does it matter?

Time,<br>position,<br>log reading,<br>course steered,<br>speed,<br>wind and weather,<br>barometer,<br>and events —<br>sail changes,<br>engine hours,<br>fixes,<br>incidents. It is the raw material for a dead reckoning if the electronics die, and it is the record if anything goes wrong.

## Preparing for a night passage: what do you do about the navigation lights, your night vision, and the watch?

Do the pilotage plan and all the chartwork in daylight. Check every navigation light and carry spare bulbs; check torches, headtorches and batteries. Set instrument lights to red and keep white deck lights off to protect night vision — 20 minutes to recover it, seconds to lose it. Everyone in lifejackets,<br>clipped on before leaving the cockpit,<br>and nobody on deck alone. Hot food and a flask made before dark,<br>the watch system briefed,<br>and the log kept every hour so a DR can be started at any moment.

## How do you register your passage details with the Coastguard?

You do not — the old CG66 voluntary safety identification scheme was withdrawn. Instead leave your plan with a responsible person ashore, or use the free <b>RYA SafeTrx</b> app, which records your intended passage and ETA and alerts your emergency contact if you are overdue. Whichever you use, tell them what to do — and what <b>not</b> to do — if you fail to arrive, and always tell them when you get in.
