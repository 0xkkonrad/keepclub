# 05 IRPCS — rules of the road

## What does IRPCS stand for, and do they apply to a small yacht?

The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea 1972, as amended — the COLREGs. They apply to <b>all</b> vessels on the high seas and connected navigable waters, whatever their size. There is no small-boat exemption from the steering and sailing rules.

## Rule 2 — the responsibility rule

Nothing in the rules excuses any vessel from the consequences of neglecting them or of neglecting the ordinary practice of seamen. And where a special circumstance or immediate danger makes it necessary, you must depart from the rules to avoid collision. Good seamanship overrides everything.

## Rule 5 — look-out

Every vessel shall at all times maintain a proper look-out by sight and hearing, and by all available means appropriate to the prevailing circumstances, so as to make a full appraisal of the situation and the risk of collision.

## Rule 6 — safe speed. What determines it?

You must always proceed at a speed at which you can take proper and effective action to avoid collision and stop within an appropriate distance. Factors: visibility,<br>traffic density,<br>your manoeuvrability,<br>background lights at night,<br>sea and wind state,<br>draught in relation to depth,<br>and the characteristics and limitations of your radar.

## Rule 7 — how do you know there is a risk of collision?

Use all available means. If the compass bearing of an approaching vessel does not appreciably change, risk of collision exists — and it can exist even with an appreciable bearing change when closing a very large vessel or a tow, or at close range. Assume risk exists if you are in any doubt, and do not rely on scanty information, especially scanty radar information.

## Rule 8 — what must action to avoid collision be like?

<b>Positive</b>,<br>made in <b>ample time</b>,<br>and with <b>due regard to good seamanship</b>. Any alteration should be large enough to be readily apparent visually or by radar; a succession of small alterations should be avoided. Prefer a course alteration if there is room. Slacken speed, stop or reverse if necessary. The action must result in passing at a safe distance, checked until finally past and clear.

## Rule 9 — narrow channels

Keep as near to the outer limit of the channel on your <b>starboard</b> side as is safe and practicable. A vessel under 20 m, or a sailing vessel, must not impede a vessel that can safely navigate only within the channel. Do not cross a channel if that impedes such a vessel. Avoid anchoring in a narrow channel.

## Rule 10: how do you use a traffic separation scheme, and what applies if you have to cross one?

Proceed in the appropriate lane in the general direction of flow, and join or leave at the termination or at as small an angle to the flow as practicable. <b>Avoid crossing lanes</b> so far as practicable; if you must cross, cross on a <b>heading as nearly as practicable at right angles</b> to the flow — a heading, so you do not offset for tide. A vessel under 20 m or a sailing vessel must not impede a power-driven vessel following a lane. Vessels under 20 m, sailing vessels and vessels fishing <b>may</b> use the inshore traffic zone.

## Rule 12 — two sailing vessels approaching

<b>Different tacks</b>: the vessel on <b>port tack</b> keeps out of the way. <b>Same tack</b>: the <b>windward</b> vessel keeps out of the way. If you are on port tack and cannot tell which tack a vessel to windward is on, keep out of her way.

## Which side is the windward side for the purposes of Rule 12?

The side <b>opposite</b> to that on which the mainsail is carried — so the opposite side to the boom. On a square-rigged vessel it is the side opposite the largest fore-and-aft sail.

## Rule 13 — overtaking

The <b>overtaking</b> vessel keeps out of the way, and remains responsible until she is <b>finally past and clear</b>. A vessel is overtaking when she comes up from more than 22.5&deg; abaft the other's beam. A subsequent change of bearing does not turn it into a crossing situation, and if in doubt assume you are overtaking. Rule 13 applies <b>notwithstanding</b> Rules 4 to 18, so it overrides the Rule 18 hierarchy: a sailing vessel overtaking a power-driven vessel must keep out of the way.

## Rule 14 — head-on situation

Two <b>power-driven</b> vessels meeting on reciprocal or nearly reciprocal courses so as to involve risk of collision: <b>each</b> alters course to <b>starboard</b> so they pass <b>port to port</b>. If in doubt whether it is a head-on situation, assume it is and act accordingly.

![diagram](ds-colregs-power.png)

## Rule 15 — crossing situation

When two power-driven vessels are crossing, the vessel which has the other on her own <b>starboard</b> side keeps out of the way and shall, if the circumstances admit, avoid crossing ahead of the other.

![diagram](ds-colregs-power.png)

## Rule 16 — the give-way vessel's duty

Take early and substantial action to keep well clear.

## Rule 17 — the stand-on vessel's duty

Keep your course and speed. But as soon as it becomes apparent that the give-way vessel is not taking appropriate action, you may take action; and when collision cannot be avoided by the give-way vessel's action alone, you <b>must</b> take the best avoiding action. In a crossing situation, a power-driven stand-on vessel should <b>not</b> alter to port for a vessel on her own port side.

## Rule 18 — the order of responsibilities between vessels

<b>Except where Rules 9, 10 and 13 otherwise require</b> — so the hierarchy is suspended in a narrow channel, in a traffic separation scheme, and when overtaking. In order, each keeping out of the way of those above: not under command;<br>restricted in ability to manoeuvre;<br>constrained by draught (international rules only, and only to be 'not impeded');<br>engaged in fishing;<br>sailing;<br>power-driven;<br>seaplanes and WIG craft.

![diagram](ds-colregs-sail.png)

## A yacht under sail with the engine running in gear meets a motor cruiser — who gives way?

Neither has priority as a sailing vessel: under Rule 3 a vessel under sail with her propelling machinery in use is a <b>power-driven vessel</b>. Rule 12 does not apply. Settle it on geometry under the power-driven rules — Rule 14 if head-on, so both alter to starboard;<br>Rule 15 if crossing, so the one with the other on her own starboard side gives way;<br>Rule 13 if overtaking.

## What day shape should a sailing vessel motor-sailing show?

A black <b>cone, point down</b>, forward where it can best be seen.

## Rule 19: who does it apply to, and what four things does it require of you?

It applies when vessels are not in sight of one another in or near an area of restricted visibility, and to <b>all</b> vessels — there is no stand-on vessel. Proceed at a safe speed adapted to the visibility; a <b>power-driven</b> vessel shall have her engines ready for immediate manoeuvre. Sound the Rule 35 fog signals. If you detect another vessel <b>by radar alone</b>, determine early whether a close-quarters situation is developing or risk of collision exists, and if so take avoiding action in ample time.

![diagram](ds-colregs-sail.png)

## Rule 19 — which alterations should you avoid?

When taking avoiding action against a vessel detected <b>by radar alone</b> (Rule 19(d)): so far as possible avoid an alteration of course <b>to port</b> for a vessel forward of the beam, other than one being overtaken, and avoid an alteration <b>towards</b> a vessel abeam or abaft the beam. Both are sound practice in fog generally.

## Rule 19 — what must you do if you hear a fog signal forward of your beam?

Unless it is determined that no risk of collision exists, reduce speed to the minimum at which you can be kept on course, and if necessary <b>take all way off</b> and in any event navigate with extreme caution until the danger of collision is over.

## Rule 20 — when must lights be shown?

From sunset to sunrise; also from sunrise to sunset in restricted visibility; and they may be exhibited at any other time when deemed necessary. <b>From sunset to sunrise</b> no other lights may be exhibited that could be mistaken for the prescribed lights, impair their visibility or distinctive character, or interfere with keeping a proper look-out. Shapes are shown by day.

## Rule 3 — what is a 'vessel not under command'?

A vessel that through some exceptional circumstance is unable to manoeuvre as required by the rules and so cannot keep out of the way — for example steering failure or a total engine breakdown.

## Rule 3 — what is a 'vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre'?

A vessel that from the nature of her work is restricted in her ability to manoeuvre as required and so cannot keep out of the way. Deemed to include: laying, servicing or picking up a navigation mark, submarine cable or pipeline;<br>dredging, surveying or underwater operations;<br>replenishment or transfer of persons, stores or cargo while underway;<br>launching or recovering aircraft;<br>mine clearance;<br>and towing where the tow severely restricts the towing vessel and her tow in their ability to deviate.

## Rule 3 — what is a 'vessel engaged in fishing'?

A vessel fishing with nets, lines, trawls or other apparatus that <b>restricts her manoeuvrability</b>. It does <b>not</b> include a vessel fishing with trolling lines, so a boat with rods has no special status.

## Rule 3 — what is meant by 'restricted visibility'?

Any condition in which visibility is restricted by fog, mist, falling snow, heavy rainstorms, sandstorms or any similar cause.

## Rule 3 — 'underway' versus 'making way'

<b>Underway</b> means not at anchor, not made fast to the shore and not aground — you can be underway and drifting with no way on. <b>Making way</b> means moving through the water. The distinction changes both your lights and your fog signal.

## A large ship is approaching in a narrow buoyed channel and you are under sail in a 10 m yacht. What do you do?

Get out of her way early — Rule 9 says a vessel under 20 m or a sailing vessel must not impede a vessel that can only navigate safely inside the channel. Move outside the channel if the depth allows, or keep to its starboard edge, well clear. Do not stand on your 'rights': you have none here.

## What does five or more short and rapid blasts mean, and what should you do on hearing them?

'I do not understand your intentions' or 'I doubt whether you are taking sufficient action to avoid collision' (Rule 34(d)), given by vessels in sight of one another and optionally supplemented by at least five short rapid flashes. Assume it is aimed at you. Take the way off or slow right down while you work out the geometry, then act decisively: if you are the give-way vessel, a large alteration to <b>starboard</b> and a substantial reduction in speed. Never alter to port for a vessel on your own port side.

## You are the stand-on vessel and the other vessel is doing nothing. What is your legal position?

Rule 17 lets you act as soon as it becomes apparent she is not taking appropriate action, and <b>requires</b> you to take the best avoiding action once collision cannot be avoided by her action alone. Standing on to the point of collision is a breach of the rules, not a defence.

## Two power-driven vessels crossing: A has B on her port bow. What must A do?

A is the <b>stand-on</b> vessel — B has A on her own starboard side, so B gives way. A holds course and speed, watches carefully, and acts under Rule 17 if B does not.

## What is the difference between the international rules and local harbour rules?

Local authorities may make byelaws and directions — speed limits,<br>prohibited areas,<br>small-craft channels,<br>precautionary areas,<br>moving-prohibited zones around large ships. They supplement the IRPCS. Check the almanac and harbour guide before you arrive, and comply with both.

## What does 'shall not impede' actually oblige you to do?

Rule 8(f). Take <b>early</b> action to leave enough sea room for the other vessel's safe passage — you must keep out of her way <b>before</b> a close-quarters situation exists. It does not give her right of way: once you are approaching so as to involve risk of collision, both of you are still bound by the ordinary steering and sailing rules, and neither is relieved of them.

## Rule 11 — when do Rules 12 to 18 apply at all?

Only when vessels are <b>in sight of one another</b>. If you cannot see her — in fog, or on radar or AIS only — Rules 12 to 18 do not apply and there is no give-way or stand-on vessel: you are in <b>Rule 19</b>, and so is she.

## You are under sail, overtaking a motor cruiser. Who keeps clear?

<b>You do.</b> Rule 13 applies notwithstanding Rules 4 to 18, so it overrides the Rule 18 hierarchy: the overtaking vessel keeps out of the way, whatever she is, until she is <b>finally past and clear</b>. Sail beats power in a crossing situation, never in an overtaking one.
