# 09 Charts and publications

## What is the difference between a small-scale and a large-scale chart?

A <b>large-scale</b> chart, such as 1:10,000, covers a small area in great detail — the harbour plan.<br>A <b>small-scale</b> chart, such as 1:150,000, covers a wide area with less detail — the passage chart. Large scale, large detail.

## Which chart should you use for pilotage into a harbour?

The <b>largest scale</b> chart available for that harbour, because it shows the most detail and the least generalisation. Plan the passage on the small-scale chart, do the entrance on the large-scale one.

## What is Admiralty Chart 5011?

The booklet 'Symbols and Abbreviations used on Admiralty Charts'. It is the dictionary for everything printed on a chart, and it is an allowed reference in the shorebased exam.

## Where do you find the chart's units of depth, its scale and its datums?

In the <b>title block</b>, together with the projection,<br>the horizontal (geodetic) datum,<br>the vertical datum for depths and for heights,<br>and the source of the survey. Read it before you use any unfamiliar chart.

## How are depths and drying heights printed on a metric chart?

Depths below chart datum are printed in metres and decimetres up to 20.9 m, e.g. 5&#8323; = 5.3 m, and in whole metres from 21 m down. A <b>drying height</b> above chart datum is printed <u>underlined</u>, e.g. <u>3</u>&#8321; = dries 3.1 m. Heights of land features and lights are in metres above MHWS.

## What do the blue and green tints on a chart mean?

<b>Green</b> marks the area that dries, between chart datum or LAT — the blue/green boundary — and <b>MHWS</b>, the green/buff boundary with the land.<br><b>Blue</b> tints mark shallow water, the darker shade shallowest, bounded by depth contours.<br><b>White</b> is deeper water.

## What is a depth contour and how do you use it in pilotage?

A line joining points of equal charted depth, for example the 5 m or 10 m contour. It is a ready-made safety line: choose a contour that clears the dangers and use the echo sounder to stay outside it — an independent check that needs no visibility.

## What does a Notice to Mariners do, and how do you apply one?

It corrects the chart for changes since printing — moved buoys, new wrecks, changed lights. Apply small corrections in <b>indelible violet ink</b>, and record the notice number and year in the bottom left margin so anyone can see how up to date the chart is.

## Name the main publications a Day Skipper should have aboard.

An almanac (Reeds or the Admiralty equivalent) with tide tables and tidal stream atlases;<br>charts at passage and harbour scale;<br>a pilot book for the area;<br>the Admiralty List of Lights and Fog Signals (NP74&ndash;84) or the almanac's light list;<br>chart 5011;<br>and up-to-date Notices to Mariners.

## What does a nautical almanac contain that you use every trip?

Tide tables for standard ports and differences for secondary ports,<br>tidal curves,<br>tidal stream information,<br>harbour plans and pilotage notes,<br>light and buoy details,<br>radio and Coastguard information,<br>distances,<br>safety and first aid sections,<br>and sunrise, sunset and twilight times.

## What is a tidal stream atlas?

A booklet of chartlets for each hour before and after HW at a standard port, showing the direction and rate of the stream by arrows, with rates given in tenths of a knot for springs and neaps, e.g. 19,34 = 1.9 kn neaps, 3.4 kn springs.

## What are tidal diamonds?

Lettered magenta diamonds on the chart marking positions where tidal stream data is tabulated. The table gives set and rate for each hour relative to HW at the reference port, for springs and for neaps.

## Chart symbols: what are these seabed abbreviations — S, M, Cy, R, Wd, Sh, G, St?

S sand,<br>M mud,<br>Cy clay,<br>R rock,<br>Wd weed,<br>Sh shells,<br>G gravel,<br>St stones. They tell you what your anchor will find.

## How does a chart show a wreck, and what makes one count as dangerous?

A wreck showing part of its hull or masts is drawn in profile. A sunken wreck counts as <b>dangerous</b> — and is enclosed in a dotted danger line — if the least depth over it is 28 m or less, or is unknown; a <b>non-dangerous</b> wreck has the same cross-hatched symbol without the danger line. The least depth is printed over the wreck if known, in a box if it has been wire-swept. Where there is a dotted danger line, the whole enclosed area is the hazard, not just the symbol.

## What is the difference between a rock that is always covered, one that dries, and one that is above water?

A rock <b>awash at chart datum</b> is shown with a specific symbol and no height;<br>a rock that <b>dries</b> carries an underlined drying height;<br>a rock <b>above MHWS</b> is drawn as an islet with a height above MHWS. Always covered rocks show the depth over them.

## What does the compass rose on a chart give you?

The true rose, and inside it the <b>magnetic</b> rose with the variation for a stated year and its annual rate of change — for example '3&deg;15'W 2020 (8'E)'.

## What are electronic charts, and what are the two main kinds?

<b>Raster</b> charts are scanned images of paper charts — everything scales together and detail degrades when you zoom.<br><b>Vector</b> charts (ENCs) are databases of objects, so layers can be turned on and off and objects interrogated, but over-zooming can present a misleadingly detailed picture from a low-detail survey.

## What are the practical limitations of a chart plotter?

Zooming in does not add survey accuracy;<br>the datum may differ from the paper chart;<br>the display shows only a fraction of the chart around you, so you lose the wider picture;<br>it depends on power and on GNSS;<br>and the operator can follow a route across a hazard hidden behind the boat symbol. Keep a paper plot as back-up.

## How often should you record a position, and where?

At regular intervals appropriate to the passage — typically hourly on a coastal passage, and more often in pilotage or near hazards — in the <b>deck log</b>, with time,<br>position,<br>log reading,<br>course steered,<br>weather and any events. If the electronics fail, the log is what lets you start a dead reckoning.

## Why keep a paper plot when the plotter is working perfectly?

Because a total electrical failure, a lightning strike or a GNSS outage leaves you with nothing but the last known position, and because plotting makes you think about the tide, the dangers and the next hour. The <b>deck log</b>, not the plot, is the record of the passage — but the plot is what makes the log usable.

## How do you tell whether the chart in front of you is up to date?

The edition date is in the bottom margin; the Notices to Mariners that have been applied are listed in the bottom <b>left-hand</b> corner in violet ink. Compare that against the current cumulative list of Notices, the almanac's supplement, or the UKHO weekly notices. A leisure chart with no corrections since a five-year-old edition is not a corrected chart.
