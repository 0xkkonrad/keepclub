# 20 Engines

## What checks do you make before starting the engine?

Engine oil level,<br>gearbox oil,<br>coolant level,<br>raw-water <b>seacock open</b>,<br>raw-water strainer clear,<br>drive belt tension and condition,<br>fuel level and no water in the filter or bowl,<br>no oil or water in the bilge under the engine,<br>gear lever in neutral,<br>and nothing loose in the engine bay.

## What do you check immediately after starting?

That cooling water is coming out with the exhaust,<br>that the oil pressure and alternator warning lights have gone out,<br>the exhaust note and colour,<br>and no unusual noise, smoke or vibration. No cooling water means stop the engine at once.

## What running checks do you make on passage?

Temperature,<br>oil pressure,<br>battery charging,<br>cooling water flow,<br>engine note and vibration,<br>fuel remaining against hours run,<br>no smell of exhaust or fuel below,<br>and a look in the engine bay for drips, chafe or a loose belt.

## What checks do you make after stopping the engine?

Let it idle briefly before shutting down if it has been working hard, then stop it, and if the boat will be left,<br>close the raw-water seacock,<br>check for leaks,<br>and top up the fuel to reduce condensation. Note the hours run in the log.

## How does a diesel engine's cooling system work on a yacht?

Almost always <b>indirect</b>: raw sea water is drawn in through the seacock and strainer, passed by an impeller pump through a heat exchanger and then out with the exhaust, where it also cools and silences it. Fresh water and antifreeze circulate round the engine itself.

## The engine overheats — what are the likely causes and what do you check first?

Loss of raw-water flow: seacock shut,<br>blocked strainer or intake,<br>weed or a plastic bag on the inlet,<br>a failed <b>impeller</b>,<br>or a slipping or broken belt. Also low coolant, or a blocked heat exchanger. Stop the engine, look at whether water is coming out of the exhaust, and work back from there. Do <b>not</b> open the fresh-water header cap while the engine is hot — scalding coolant will blow out.

## Why does the engine stop or lose power, and what do you check?

Almost always <b>fuel</b>: empty tank,<br>blocked pre-filter or fine filter,<br>water or diesel bug in the fuel,<br>air in the system,<br>a shut-off fuel cock,<br>or a stopped lift pump. Check the filter bowl for water and dirt, change the filter, and bleed the system.

## How do you bleed air out of a diesel fuel system?

Fill and refit the filters, then bleed each point in turn working from the tank towards the injectors —<br><b>pre-filter,<br>lift pump,<br>fine filter,<br>then the injection pump</b> — operating the manual lift-pump lever until fuel runs out clear and bubble-free, closing each screw before moving on. If it still will not start, slacken the injector unions <b>half a turn only</b> and crank in bursts of no more than 10 seconds, keeping hands and face clear: fuel at injection pressure can inject through skin and cause a serious injury. Many modern engines self-bleed — check the manual first.

## Why must a diesel engine be bled, when a petrol engine need not?

Because the injection system relies on very high pressure and cannot compress the air, so an air lock stops fuel reaching the cylinder altogether. Air gets in when the tank runs dry, a filter is changed, or a joint leaks.

## What routine maintenance does a yacht diesel need?

Oil and oil filter change at the manufacturer's interval,<br>fuel pre-filter and fine filter,<br>air filter,<br>impeller (carry a spare),<br>anodes in the heat exchanger,<br>drive belt,<br>coolant strength,<br>gearbox oil,<br>stern gland grease or seal check,<br>and battery terminals and electrolyte.

## How do you adjust the tension of the alternator drive belt, and how tight is right?

Slacken the alternator mounting and adjusting bolts, lever the alternator out, and retighten. Correct tension gives about 10 mm of deflection at the middle of the longest run when pushed firmly with a thumb. Too loose and it slips and the water pump stops; too tight and you wreck the bearings.

## How do you estimate fuel consumption and range?

For a small yacht diesel, a rough rule is <b>about 1 litre per hour for every 10 hp</b> at cruising revs; better still, measure your own by hours run against litres put in. Range = usable tank capacity / consumption per hour &times; speed, and never plan to use the last quarter of the tank.

## What is prop walk (paddle-wheel effect), and which way does it push?

<b>Paddle-wheel effect</b> (prop walk): the propeller pushes the stern sideways, most noticeably in astern. A right-handed propeller in astern walks the stern to <b>port</b>. Learn which way yours goes and use it to bring the stern in when berthing.

## What is 'prop wash' and how do you use it?

The stream of water the propeller throws over the rudder. Ahead, it gives the rudder bite, so a brief burst of ahead power with the helm over will turn the boat even at a standstill. In astern there is no prop wash over the rudder, so steering astern needs way on.

## Why should you check for lines in the water before starting the engine?

Because a mooring line, sheet or MOB retrieval line round the propeller stops the engine, may damage the shaft and gland, and leaves you without power at the worst moment. It is the standard mistake in a man-overboard drill.

## What is a stern gland and why check it?

The seal where the propeller shaft leaves the hull. A traditional packed gland should drip slowly when running and be greased regularly; a modern lip or face seal should not drip at all. A dry traditional gland overheats; a leaking one floods the bilge.

## What do black, blue and white exhaust smoke suggest?

<b>Black</b> — incomplete combustion: overloading, a dirty air filter, a fouled propeller or bottom.<br><b>Blue</b> — burning lubricating oil: worn engine.<br><b>White</b> — water vapour or unburnt fuel: a cold engine, water in the fuel, or in the worst case a head gasket.

## The starter turns the engine but it will not fire — what is your sequence?

Check the stop control is in, the fuel cock open and the tank has fuel;<br>look for water or air in the filter;<br>feel whether the glow plugs or cold-start aid have been used;<br>check the throttle is not at idle stop;<br>then bleed the fuel system. Do not keep cranking — you will flatten the battery and fill the exhaust with fuel.

## You have picked up a rope round the propeller. What do you do?

Engine into neutral and stop it at once — take the key out so nobody restarts it. Sail or anchor clear of danger. Try to unwind it from on deck with a boat hook, turning the shaft by hand if you can reach it. Only go in the water in calm, sheltered conditions,<br>engine stopped and key removed,<br>tethered,<br>with someone watching,<br>and with a mask and a serrated knife — never at sea or in a stream. Otherwise anchor or take a tow and deal with it in harbour.

## How do you change a raw-water impeller, and what else must you check?

Shut the raw-water seacock. Undo the pump cover plate,<br>keep the gasket or O-ring,<br>and pull the impeller out with pliers or an extractor. Fit the new one the right way round with a smear of glycerine,<br>refit the cover,<br>open the seacock,<br>then start up and confirm water is coming out with the exhaust. <b>Account for every broken vane</b> — any left in the system will be jammed in the heat exchanger and will overheat you again. Carry a spare impeller, gasket and the right spanner.

## What tools and spares should be aboard a cruising yacht?

Impeller and gasket,<br>fuel pre-filter and fine filter,<br>engine oil and an extractor pump,<br>drive belt,<br>gearbox oil,<br>jubilee clips,<br>self-amalgamating and duct tape,<br>spare fuses and bulbs,<br>wire and terminals,<br>softwood bungs,<br>sail-repair tape,<br>needle and thread,<br>spare shackles and blocks,<br>spare line,<br>hose clips,<br>bolt croppers or a hacksaw for the rigging,<br>spanners and sockets,<br>screwdrivers,<br>mole grips,<br>a sharp knife,<br>a torch —<br>and the engine manual.
