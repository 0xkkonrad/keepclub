# 04 Safety equipment and fire

## What safety brief should every new crew member get before you leave?

Where the lifejackets, harnesses and clipping points are and how to fit them;<br>where the flares, fire extinguishers, fire blanket, first aid kit, liferaft and grab bag are;<br>how to work the heads, gas and galley;<br>how to send a VHF distress alert;<br>the MOB drill;<br>the location of seacocks and bilge pumps;<br>and who does what on deck.

## Lifejacket or buoyancy aid: what is the difference, and what rating do you want for cruising?

A <b>lifejacket</b> — 100 N is the lowest lifejacket level, for sheltered water with prompt rescue at hand; 150 N for coastal and offshore use; 275 N for extreme conditions or heavy clothing — is designed to turn an unconscious wearer face-up and support them. A <b>buoyancy aid</b> is <b>50 N</b>, inshore, and only helps a conscious person stay afloat: it will <b>not</b> turn an unconscious person face-up. Cruising calls for at least 150 N, with a crotch strap, light, whistle, spray hood and preferably an AIS beacon or PLB.

## When should the crew wear lifejackets and harnesses?

Whenever the skipper says so — and as a minimum at night,<br>in poor visibility,<br>in rough weather,<br>when reefing or working on deck,<br>in a dinghy,<br>and for non-swimmers and children. Clip on before you leave the cockpit at night.

## What is a danbuoy for?

It marks a man overboard with a visible flag on a weighted pole, often with a light, drogue and horseshoe lifebuoy. It gives the helm something to steer back to — a head in the water is almost invisible at any distance.

## Red parachute rocket flare, red hand flare, orange smoke, white flare — what is each for?

<b>Red parachute rocket</b> — distress, to attract attention over the horizon at night, burns high for about 40 seconds.<br><b>Red hand flare</b> — distress, to pinpoint your position for a rescuer who is already close.<br><b>Orange smoke</b> — distress by day, best for aircraft and helicopters.<br><b>White flare</b> — <b>not</b> a distress signal: it warns another vessel of your presence to avoid collision.

## How do you fire a hand flare, and how do you fire a parachute rocket?

Read the instructions in daylight, before you need them. <b>Hand flare or smoke:</b> hold it at arm's length over the side on the <b>downwind, leeward</b> side, angled away from you, the boat and the rig, gloves on, and do not look at it. <b>Parachute rocket:</b> stand with your back to the wind and fire it near vertically, angled about 15&deg; downwind in strong wind — never held out over the water, and never near the rig or the liferaft. Under low cloud a rocket disappears into the cloud, so use a hand flare instead. Never let anyone look down the tube.

## What fire extinguishers should a cruising yacht carry, and where?

At least two multi-purpose dry powder extinguishers stowed by the exits — typically one at the companionway and one at the forward end of the accommodation — plus a <b>fire blanket</b> in the galley and ideally an automatic or port-fed extinguisher for the engine compartment. Check the gauges and service dates.

## How do you fight a galley fat fire?

Turn off the gas at the bottle or isolator, smother the pan with a <b>fire blanket</b>, and leave it covered to cool. Never use water on burning fat and never carry a burning pan through the boat.

## How do you fight an engine compartment fire?

Stop the engine, shut off the fuel, and discharge the extinguisher through the dedicated port <b>without</b> opening the compartment — opening it feeds the fire with air. Then leave the space closed and cool it from outside.

## The four elements of fire prevention on a yacht

Control the fuel (gas, diesel, petrol, spirits, oily rags),<br>control ignition (galley, electrics, smoking, engine),<br>keep the boat clean and dry in the bilge and engine bay,<br>and check installations — gas hoses, jubilee clips, wiring, exhaust lagging — before the season and on passage.

## Why is LPG particularly dangerous on a boat, and how is the risk managed?

Butane and propane are heavier than air, so a leak sinks into the bilge and forms an explosive pocket that will not ventilate out. Manage it with the bottle in a self-draining locker vented overboard,<br>a shut-off at the bottle and at the cooker,<br>an in-date flexible hose,<br>a bubble tester or leak detector,<br>and a habit of turning gas off at the bottle when not cooking.

## What goes in a grab bag?

Handheld VHF,<br>EPIRB or PLB,<br>handheld GNSS,<br>flares,<br>torch,<br>whistle,<br>water,<br>high-energy food,<br>first aid and seasickness tablets,<br>thermal protective aids,<br>sun protection,<br>a knife,<br>a hand bailer,<br>spare glasses and any essential medication,<br>plus passports and ship's papers if there is time.

## When do you actually get into the liferaft?

Only when you are certain you must step <b>up</b> into it — the yacht is your best liferaft until she is definitely sinking or on fire beyond control. Send a distress alert first, put on lifejackets, take the grab bag, and never cut the painter until everyone is aboard.

## How do you launch and board a liferaft?

Secure the painter to a strong point on the boat,<br>throw the raft over the leeward side,<br>pull the painter hard to fire the gas bottle,<br>board dry from the deck if you can,<br>get everyone in,<br>then stream the drogue,<br>close the canopy,<br>cut the painter and bail out. Take seasickness tablets early.

## What is a radar reflector for and why does it matter in fog?

It makes a small glassfibre yacht visible on a ship's radar. Mount it as high as possible and the right way up. It is not a guarantee — a yacht is a poor radar target, and in poor visibility you should assume you have not been seen.

## EPIRB versus PLB versus SART versus AIS beacon

<b>EPIRB</b> — 406 MHz beacon registered to the vessel, alerts search and rescue via satellite worldwide.<br><b>PLB</b> — the same idea, registered to a person and carried on them.<br><b>SART</b> — radar transponder that paints a line of blips on a searching ship's radar.<br><b>AIS beacon</b> — alerts nearby AIS-equipped vessels, ideal for a man overboard.

## What lifesaving gear should you check before every passage?

Lifejackets fitted and serviced,<br>harnesses and tethers,<br>jackstays,<br>lifebuoy and danbuoy free to release,<br>liferaft in date and lashed with a hydrostatic release or slip,<br>flares in date,<br>extinguishers charged,<br>first aid kit stocked,<br>bilge pumps working,<br>torch batteries good,<br>and softwood bungs to hand.

## How do you deal with a crew member who is seriously seasick?

Take it seriously: it causes dehydration, poor judgement and accidents. Get them on deck in the fresh air,<br>low down and warm,<br>looking at the horizon,<br>with something in their stomach and a job to do such as steering. Clip them on,<br>keep fluids going,<br>and treat them as a casualty, not a nuisance. Tablets work best taken well before you sail.

## Cold water shock — what happens and what do you do?

Sudden immersion causes an involuntary gasp, hyperventilation, a spike in heart rate and loss of grip and swimming ability within a couple of minutes. <b>In the water: float to live</b> — fight the instinct to thrash, lean back with your head back and airway clear, sculling gently, for 60 to 90 seconds until the gasping passes; then shout for help or swim.<br><b>As rescuer:</b> get them out fast, keep them <b>horizontal</b>, insulate them, no alcohol, no rubbing the limbs, handle them gently to avoid post-immersion collapse, and get medical help.

## Hypothermia — the signs and the treatment

Shivering,<br>then pale cold skin,<br>slurred speech,<br>clumsiness,<br>confusion,<br>apathy and finally collapse. Handle very gently,<br>get them into shelter,<br>remove wet clothing,<br>insulate the whole body including the head,<br>warm drinks only if fully conscious,<br>no alcohol,<br>no rubbing,<br>no hot bath,<br>and call for medical help early.

## What is a jackstay and how should a tether be used?

A jackstay is a webbing or wire line running fore and aft along the deck. Clip the harness tether to it so you can move without unclipping. Keep the tether short enough that you cannot go over the side, and clip on inside the cockpit before you go out on deck.

## Minimum first aid you should be able to do at sea

Assess response, airway, breathing and circulation;<br>control severe bleeding with direct pressure;<br>treat shock, burns, hypothermia and near-drowning;<br>recognise when to call for medical advice by VHF (a PAN-PAN to the Coastguard) and how to describe symptoms clearly.

## Are flares a legal requirement, and what are the modern alternatives?

Pyrotechnic flares are <b>not</b> legally required for UK pleasure vessels under 13.7 m; they are compulsory for vessels of 13.7 m and over. Many cruisers now rely on a VHF/DSC set with a correctly registered MMSI,<br>an EPIRB or PLB,<br>an AIS beacon on each lifejacket,<br>a mobile phone,<br>and an electronic visual distress signal. If you do carry flares, everyone aboard must know where they are and how to fire them — and you own the disposal problem.

## What is a hydrostatic release unit and why does the liferaft need one?

A link that holds the liferaft in its cradle but releases automatically at about 4 m of water depth, so the raft frees itself if the yacht sinks before anyone can launch it. Lash the raft with the HRU rather than with rope,<br>check its expiry date,<br>and make sure the painter is made fast to a strong point on the <b>boat</b>, not to the cradle.
