# 1. `cc-ropework` — Ropework

The syllabus bar: *"Handling ropes, including coiling, stowing, securing to cleats and single
and double bollards. Handling warps. Ability to tie the following knots and to know their
correct use: figure-of-eight, clove hitch, rolling hitch, bowline, round turn and two half
hitches, single and double sheet bend, reef knot."*

Note the wording — **"and to know their correct use"**. The examinable content is as much
*which knot, when* as it is the tying. That is what the material below is organised around.

## 1.1 Figure-of-eight

**What it is for.** A stopper knot. Source opened
(https://www.animatedknots.com/figure-8-knot): it is *"a quick and convenient stopper knot to
prevent a line sliding out of sight, e.g., up inside the mast."*

**On a yacht, concretely** — from https://yachtsailtraining.com/2026/02/15/essential-rya-knots-guide/:
tied in the ends of **sheets and halyards** to stop them running out through blocks, clutches or
fairleads. That page's extra practical point: it is *"visually inspectable from the cockpit"* —
you can see from the helm whether the crew put one in.

**Why the figure-of-eight and not a simple overhand knot.** Animated Knots is explicit: the
figure-8 *"doesn't bind; it can be undone easily"* even after being jammed hard up against a
block, and it is *"much better than the simple Overhand Knot which is smaller and can bind so
tightly that it can be really difficult to undo."* This is the neighbour-comparison card for
this knot.

**The common failure.** Same page: *"The Figure 8 Knot can fall undone and then has to be
retied."* The very property that makes it good (it does not bind) means it works loose when
unloaded. It needs checking, not trusting.

**Mnemonic.** No genuine traditional mnemonic exists for this one. Do not invent one. The
shape itself ("over, under, through — it looks like an 8") is the memory aid, and it is
descriptive rather than a mnemonic.

## 1.2 Clove hitch

**What it is for.** The honest answer from https://www.animatedknots.com/clove-hitch-knot-rope-end
is: **fenders, and not much else.** That page's stated boat use is attaching a fender line to a
guardrail/railing — *"initially secured with a Clove Hitch and then, when adjusted, secured with
two Half Hitches round the standing end."* Its other listed use is theatrical scenery bars,
because the knot can be rolled up or down the bar to adjust height.

**The virtue.** It is fast and it is **adjustable** — you can slide it along a rail to set fender
height. https://yachtsailtraining.com/2026/02/15/essential-rya-knots-guide/ gives the same use:
*"quick temporary securing of fenders when coming alongside a jetty; easily adjustable."*

**The common failure — and this one is strongly worded.** Animated Knots:

> *"It does have two giant faults: it slips and, paradoxically, can also bind. It should be
> deeply distrusted when used by itself."*

and

> *"If on a boat you feel an urge to use a Clove Hitch – resist! Choose something else unless you
> are merely hanging a fender."*

It also warns that "fixing" it by stacking extra half hitches on top *"is inviting a major strain
to cause the earlier turns to bind tightly and become impossible to untie."* The
yachtsailtraining page agrees more mildly: it *"can slip on smooth rope or with inconsistent
tension"*, and for tidal or high-wind situations recommends a round turn and two half hitches
instead.

**Card-worthy contrast:** clove hitch = temporary, adjustable, low-load, *watched*. Anything
that must hold unattended or under real load → round turn and two half hitches.

**Second failure mode worth a card:** the clove hitch comes undone if the object it is tied to
**rotates** (a rolling fender, a spinning rail). That follows from its structure — it is two
opposing turns held by friction against each other.

## 1.3 Rolling hitch — the one the syllabus really cares about

**What it is for.** From https://www.animatedknots.com/rolling-hitch-knot, the two stated uses,
both maritime:

> *"to take the strain off a rope with a foul turn on a winch"*
> *"to relieve the strain on a hawser while the 'Bitter End' is transferred to the 'Bitts'"*

It is also the basis of the Midshipman's Hitch used for *"spring lines or boom-vangs on boats."*

**The classic yacht job — the riding turn / winch override.** This is the card. Sequence
(described in the same terms on https://www.animatedknots.com/rolling-hitch-knot and in the
Yachting Monthly riding-turn piece at
https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/free-riding-turn-2-35132):

1. A riding turn (override) has locked the sheet solid on the winch — the tail has jammed under
   a loaded turn and you cannot ease or release it.
2. Tie a **rolling hitch** in a spare line onto the **loaded part of the jammed sheet**, forward
   of the winch.
3. Lead that spare line to **another winch** and take up the tension until the load transfers off
   the jammed sheet.
4. With the sheet now slack, unwind the riding turn.
5. Take the load back onto the original winch.

**Why the rolling hitch and not a clove hitch — the neighbour comparison.** Structurally a rolling
hitch is a clove hitch with an extra riding turn tucked in, which means it grips **lengthwise
along** the rope or spar it is tied to. Animated Knots states the constraint as a warning:

> *"The 'pull' MUST be in line with the main rope (or the pole). If the tension is away from the
> standing rope or pole, this knot is likely to fail."*

and, on reliability under load:

> *"The Rolling Hitch is one of the few knots which can be tied and untied with load on. It does
> not bind and, when tied correctly, does not slip."*

A clove hitch in the same place would slide. So: **clove hitch = pull at right angles, light
load; rolling hitch = pull along the line, and it can be tied and released with load on it.**

**Two extra points from the same page.**
- The two turns must both go on the **same side** — the side the load will come from. A rolling
  hitch tied "the wrong way round" slides off. (The page describes the tuck *"between the standing
  end and the first turn"*, which is what sets the direction.)
- Modern slippery rope: *"Some modern ropes are very slippery, e.g., Spectra®, Dyneema®, and
  Polypropylene. A Rolling Hitch will not hold at all in such materials."* Relevant on a modern
  boat with Dyneema sheets — a real limitation, not a footnote.

**Mnemonic.** None traditional. The *structure* is the memory: "a clove hitch with one extra turn
on the loaded side."

## 1.4 Bowline

**What it is for.** From https://www.animatedknots.com/bowline-knot:

> *"The Bowline makes a reasonably secure loop in the end of a piece of rope. It has many uses,
> e.g., to fasten a mooring line to a ring or a post."*

Concretely on a yacht — from https://yachtsailtraining.com/2026/02/15/essential-rya-knots-guide/:
**bending the jib sheets onto the clew of the headsail**, and any time you need a fixed loop that
will not tighten: a loop over a bollard, a mooring line to a ring, a line round a crew member.

**Why the bowline over anything else — the property that matters.** It *"performs well under load
without slipping or binding and can be easily untied when not loaded."* yachtsailtraining puts it
as *"famous for being easy to undo, even after it has been subjected to immense tension."*
That is the whole point: unlike a knot that jams, a bowline lets go afterwards.

**The mnemonic — this is the genuine one.** The rabbit and the tree. Standard wording:

> The rabbit comes **up** out of the hole, **round** the back of the tree, and back **down** the
> hole.

The yachtsailtraining page confirms the mnemonic is in RYA-school use, referring to *"the rabbit
out of the hole"* story as the memory aid. The exact wording varies between schools ("round the
tree" vs "round the back of the tree"); the three-move structure is constant.

**The common failures.** From Animated Knots:
- **It shakes loose when it is not loaded.** *"When not under tension, this knot can readily work
  itself untied."* This is the reason a bowline on a sheet gets a good long tail, and the reason
  some schools teach a stopper or a locking turn in the tail.
- **It cannot be tied or untied under significant tension on the standing end** — so it is the
  wrong knot when you may need an emergency release under load.
- Tail length: for intermittent loads some sources want a tail *"12 times the rope
  circumference, though this is rarely practiced."* Useful only as "leave a generous tail".
- Not for climbing / life support without a backup knot. Out of CC scope but explains why the
  knot has a reputation.

**Extra usable fact:** two bowlines can be linked loop-through-loop to join two ropes — Animated
Knots states this explicitly. That is an acceptable way to join two lines, unlike a reef knot
(§1.8).

## 1.5 Round turn and two half hitches

**What it is for.** From https://www.animatedknots.com/round-turn-two-half-hitches-knot:
*"very useful for attaching a rope to a ring, bar, pole, or dock post."* yachtsailtraining calls
it the *"bread and butter"* knot for securing a boat to a ring or a pole and notes it is
*"essential for Mediterranean mooring."*

**Why the round turn is the point of the knot.** Animated Knots:

> *"[the initial round turn] should take the initial strain while you complete the knot."*

That is the card. The two turns round the post hold the load by friction, so you can let the
weight of the boat sit on the round turn while your hands are free to tie the half hitches. It is
the knot you can tie **with the line already loaded** — hence yachtsailtraining's *"incredibly
secure and can be tied while the line is under tension."*

**The scaling rule.** Same page: *"An additional turn, or even two additional turns, should be
added initially if you are handling a heavy load, e.g., with a large vessel or in a strong
wind."* And: *"use as many turns on the post as are necessary to control the strain."* This is a
genuinely crew-level skill: more turns = more friction = more control.

**Practical detail.** *"Always tie the Half Hitches in the same 'direction.'"* Two half hitches
tied the same way form a clove hitch round the standing part; tied opposite ways they form a
cow hitch, which is weaker and looks wrong. Also worth learning to tie half hitches one-handed so
the other hand can hold the strain.

**Common failure.** Half hitches tied in opposite directions; too few turns for the load so the
line surges while you are tying; and hitches tied round the wrong part so they cannot be undone.

**Neighbour comparison for a card.** Bowline vs round turn and two half hitches on a mooring ring:
a bowline gives a **fixed loop** you can lift off, but cannot be tied or released under load; the
round turn and two half hitches **can** be tied under load and can be eased, but does not give you
a loop to slip off a bollard.

## 1.6 Single sheet bend

**What it is for.** From https://www.animatedknots.com/sheet-bend-knot:
*"The Sheet Bend … is recommended for joining two ropes of unequal size."* It works with equal
sizes too, but unequal is its speciality — that is precisely why it exists and why the reef knot
does not do this job.

**On a yacht:** joining two mooring warps to make a long line, bending a flag halyard, bending a
messenger line onto a heavier warp. The name is nautical: it comes from bending a sheet to the
clew of a sail.

**The two rules that make it work** — both on that page:
1. **The thicker or stiffer rope forms the bight**; the thinner rope goes through it and round.
   Get this backwards and it can capsize.
2. **Both tails must finish on the same side of the knot.** Verbatim: *"When correctly tied the
   two tails of the Sheet Bend lie on the same side of the knot. The alternative version – with
   the tails on opposite sides – is less reliable."* Same-side is the check you can teach a crew
   member to perform by eye.

**Common failures.** Tails on opposite sides (the "left-handed sheet bend"); the wrong rope
forming the bight; short tails. Animated Knots: *"For critical loads the ends must be left much
longer."* The knot also needs tension to be reliable — like a bowline it can shake loose when
slack.

## 1.7 Double sheet bend

**When you choose it over the single.** Verbatim from the same page:

> *"when the ropes are markedly different in size, the tail of the smaller rope can be taken twice
> round the bight in the larger rope to create the double sheet bend"*

— i.e. the double is not a "better" knot in general, it is the version for a **large size
mismatch**, and by extension for slippery or stiff rope where the single would slip. Second turn,
same direction, alongside the first, tails still ending the same side.

## 1.8 Reef knot

**What it is for.** From https://www.animatedknots.com/square-knot: securing something around
itself — *"tie a sail cover over a sail; you can tie the string on a gift; and you can tie the
laces on your shoes."* On a yacht that means: **reef points / sail ties round a stowed sail, a
sail cover, a bundle**. It is a *binding* knot, not a bend.

**Why it earns its place in the syllabus** — the name. It is the knot for tying in a reef,
because it lies flat against the sail and can be spilled and untied quickly, even after being
loaded, by capsizing it.

**The mnemonic.** *"Left over right, right over left"* — confirmed in that phrasing at
https://yachtsailtraining.com/2026/02/15/essential-rya-knots-guide/. Fuller traditional version:
"left over right and under, right over left and under." The point of the mnemonic is that the two
half knots must be tied in **opposite** directions.

**The failure — and this is the strongest warning of all seven knots.** Animated Knots:

> *"There have probably been more lives lost as a result of using a Square Knot as a bend (to tie
> two ropes together) than from the failure of any other half dozen knots combined."*

and *"**Never** use it for critical loads."* The knot *"slips, it comes undone, it jams"*, and
even a stack of them *"can capsize and pull undone."*

**The granny knot.** Same page: *"it is all too easy to tie a Granny instead which behaves even
less well."* A granny is what you get if you tie both half knots the same way round. Recognition
test for a card: a correctly tied reef knot lies **flat**, with each rope's standing part and tail
emerging together on the same side of its own bight; a granny sits **cocked at an angle** and jams.

**The card-level rule to carry away:** *a reef knot ties a rope around an object, back to itself.
It never joins two separate ropes.* For that, sheet bend (unequal), double sheet bend (very
unequal), or two linked bowlines.

## 1.9 Cleating a line

Source opened: https://www.animatedknots.com/cleat-hitch-knot-dock-line

**The sequence.**
1. **Lead the line to the far horn first.** Verbatim: *"Initially the rope must be led round the
   most distant horn of the cleat followed by a turn in the same direction round the other horn."*
   The page states that starting with the near horn *"risks jamming."*
2. **Figure-of-eight turns.** Cross diagonally over the centre of the cleat, under the opposite
   horn, back across. *"The Figure 8 Turns lift the rope up against the horns and out of the way
   of the first turn."*
3. **Do not make a full round turn to start with.** *"Never make a complete round turn initially;
   this prevents catastrophic jamming when loads shift."*

**How many turns.** The page's judgement: modern slippery rope needs **more** turns than old
books show; *"two crossovers is minimal for temporary use only"*; add turns for storm loads.

**The locking hitch — and when NOT to use one. This is the disagreement card.**
Animated Knots' position is a clean split:

- **No locking hitch:** *"Large vessels, towlines, and sail sheets should never include a final
  half hitch — rapid release capability is essential."*
- **Locking hitch acceptable:** *"Long-term dock lines, halyards, and flag lines may safely
  include a locking hitch for extra security."*

The reasoning: a locking hitch is a deliberate jam. If the line has to be released **now** —
a sheet, a towline, a big boat's warp with the tide coming on — you cannot afford a knot that
has to be picked out under load.

> **Genuine practice disagreement — flag it in the card, do not resolve it.**
> The RYA is commonly taught as **"OXO"**: a round turn (O), a figure-of-eight (X), a final round
> turn (O) — i.e. finish with a plain turn, **no** locking hitch. Many British instructors go
> further and hold that *a locking turn has no place on a boat above dinghy size*, on the grounds
> that if a towline goes momentarily slack the first turn can lift off the horn and clamp on top
> of the locking turn, making it impossible to release under load. The counter-position — the
> figure-8-plus-locking-hitch taught almost universally in North America — is that without the
> hitch a slippery modern line can shake loose. Sources for the existence of the disagreement:
> the discussion at https://forums.ybw.com/threads/locking-turn-or-oxxo-on-a-cleat.152302/ and
> https://forums.ybw.com/threads/rya-method-for-making-fast-to-a-cleat.156449/ (forums, so
> **evidence that the disagreement exists**, not authority for either side), plus the
> Animated Knots split above.
> **What I could not verify:** I could not open an RYA page that states the OXO method in the
> RYA's own words. Treat "the RYA teaches OXO" as *widely reported* rather than *cited*.

**Two more cleating points worth cards.**
- Leave the tail lying **fair** and free — a cleated line that has to be let go in a hurry must
  not be buried under a coil.
- Never cleat a line with the tail leading back over the top of the loaded part in a way that
  traps it.

## 1.10 Coiling and stowing

**Lay matters.** Three-strand rope is almost universally right-laid and must be coiled
**clockwise**, otherwise it kinks and hockles. Modern braided line has no lay and can be coiled
in figure-of-eights to avoid building in twist. Source: https://www.cruisingworld.com/how-coil-halyard/
(*caveat: I have this from the search engine's extraction of the page rather than a clean direct
fetch — treat the clockwise/right-laid rule as solidly conventional but re-open the page before
quoting it verbatim*).

**The halyard coil hung on its own cleat.** The standard method: coil the halyard, reach through
the coil, pull a bight of the standing part through, twist it, and drop the loop over the cleat
horn. The coil then hangs from the *loaded* part of the halyard, so it can be shaken free and let
run without the coil having to be taken off first.

**Why a halyard tail gets coiled and hung, not dropped.** Two reasons, both card-worthy and both
practical rather than sourced-quotable: a loose halyard tail in the cockpit or on the sidedeck is
a trip hazard and can go over the side and round the propeller; and a coiled tail can be let run
cleanly when the sail has to come down in a hurry.

**Halyards at the mast.** They are commonly **flaked** in figure-eights on deck or dropped into a
halyard bag rather than coiled, precisely so they run free when the sail is hoisted or dropped.

**Coiling a mooring warp for stowage.** Coil clockwise, then with the last two or three feet make
three turns *around* the upper part of the coil, form a loop in the last of the line and tuck it
through the top of the coil above the turns.

## 1.11 Securing to single and double bollards

**Single bollard (a post).** Two ways, and the difference is a good card:
- **Round turn and two half hitches** — can be tied under load, can be eased under load, does not
  come off until you undo it.
- **Bowline dropped over the post** — a fixed loop you can lift straight off. Faster to let go,
  but cannot be tied or adjusted while loaded.

**Double bollard / samson post with two horns (or a bollard with a cross-bar).** Take a figure-of-
eight round the two horns and finish with a turn; the principle is the same as a cleat.

**"Dipping the eye" — the classic double-up rule.** When your mooring line has a spliced eye and
someone else's eye is already on the bollard, do **not** simply drop yours on top. Instead pass
your eye **up through** theirs from underneath, then over the bollard. Both eyes then sit on the
post but either boat can lift its own eye off without disturbing the other's.
Source: https://igkt-solent.co.uk/dipping-the-eye/ and
https://engineeringtraining.tpub.com/14160/Dipping-The-Eye-89.html
(*caveat: I have both of these from the search engine's page extraction rather than a clean direct
fetch. The technique is entirely standard and both pages describe it identically, but re-open
before quoting.*)
The failure it prevents: the boat that arrived first is trapped, because its eye is pinned under
the second boat's eye.

## 1.12 Warps — the names

The four names the syllabus's "handling warps" implies. Sources:
https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/how-to-moor-a-yacht-securely-73706 and
https://jimmygreen.com/content/52-mooring-warp-length-and-configuration (*both via search
extraction — see caveat; the naming is uncontroversial and consistent across every source I saw*).

| warp | runs from | stops the boat |
|---|---|---|
| **bow line** | bow, forward to the pontoon | drifting aft and off at the bow |
| **stern line** | stern, aft to the pontoon | drifting forward and off at the stern |
| **breast line** | bow or stern, **perpendicular** to the pontoon | moving *sideways away* from the pontoon |
| **spring** | roughly amidships, led well forward or well aft | moving **fore and aft** along the berth |

**Springs are the ones crew get wrong**, so they deserve the card:
- **Fore spring** (a.k.a. forward spring): leads **forward** from the boat to a cleat ahead —
  stops the boat moving **aft**.
- **Back spring** (a.k.a. aft spring): leads **aft** to a cleat behind — stops the boat moving
  **forward**.
- Mnemonic that actually holds: *a spring is named for the direction it leads, and it stops the
  boat going the other way.* (Note honestly: some sources name a spring for the movement it
  prevents instead, so the names are **not universally used the same way**. Say "the one leading
  forward" if there is any doubt — that is unambiguous and is what a skipper will accept.)

**Lengths, useful practical numbers:** bow and stern lines at least half the boat's length,
preferably longer; springs the full length of the boat or longer, because a long line stretches
and absorbs surge, a short one snatches.

**Breast lines are temporary.** They hold the boat in tight against the pontoon for loading, but
they take shock badly and should not be left as the sole mooring — a rising or falling tide will
either lift the boat against them or leave it hanging.

# 2. `cc-tender` — Tender usage / dinghies

The syllabus bar: *"Understands and complies with the loading rules. Is able to handle a dinghy
under oars. Understand the use and importance of the kill cord. Understand safety equipment for
tenders."*

Four sub-topics, explicitly. The kill cord is one quarter of the section by weight.

## 2.1 Loading rules and the maximum-persons plate

**The plate.** UK/EU recreational craft carry a **builder's plate** which, under the Recreational
Craft Regulations, must show the manufacturer's **maximum recommended load** (in kg — fuel, water,
stores, gear *and people*) and the **maximum number of persons** the craft is designed to carry
when under way. The figures are set by the builder from the craft's design category, stability
and freeboard.
Sources: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1996/1353/schedule/1/made and
https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2004/1464/schedule/1/made (*caveat: I could only read these
through the search engine's extraction — the current 2017 consolidation at
`legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2017/737/schedule/1/made` serves the schedule as **scanned images**, so I
could not read the operative words directly. The maximum-load/maximum-persons requirement is
consistently described the same way in every derived source I saw, but it is not a verbatim
citation.*)

**The rules, in the form a crew member needs them.**
- The plate figure is a **maximum, not a target**. It is a flat-water, good-conditions number.
- It counts **everything**: people, outboard, fuel, water cans, shopping, kit bags.
- If it is choppy, cold, dark, or you are going any distance, **carry less than the plate says**.
- **Make two trips.** From https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/use-tender-safely-60768:
  *"Labouring across an anchorage with an inch of freeboard is likely to end both damply and
  unhappily."* Overloading a **rigid** tender *"can be properly dangerous"* because rigid tenders
  swamp — an inflatable's tubes give it reserve buoyancy a hard dinghy simply does not have.
- The RNLI's stated position (via https://uk.boats.com/how-to/kill-cord-reminder-rnli-video-of-runaway-powerboat/
  and https://www.boatsandoutboards.co.uk/resources/safety-at-sea-advice-from-the-rnli/, both
  reporting RNLI guidance — *the RNLI's own page 403s to automated fetch*): overloading is one of
  the biggest causes of tender incidents because it leads to **swamping**; make extra runs,
  especially in poor weather.

**Freeboard is the check you can teach.** Freeboard = the vertical distance from the water to the
top of the tender's side (the gunwale or the top of the tube). It is the whole margin against a
wave or a wash coming aboard. The rule of thumb a crew member can apply without a calculation:
*if the freeboard has gone, so has your safety margin — take someone or something out.*
The failure mode is not capsize, it is **swamping** — filling from a wake or a chop and losing
stability and buoyancy at once.

**Trim.** From https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/handling-rowing-a-dinghy-a-beginners-guide-88389:
avoid putting people on the **side tubes**; put them on the transom or the seat. Too much weight
forward *"trims the bow down"* and the tender ploughs; too much aft and it squats and steers
badly. Keep the load **low and central**.

**Do not wear your load.** Same source (use-tender-safely): stow bags **on the sole**, do not wear
them. A worn backpack *"initially floats but eventually floods and pulls you under"* if you go
in — a specific, memorable, card-worthy hazard.

## 2.2 Boarding a tender

Sources: https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/handling-rowing-a-dinghy-a-beginners-guide-88389
and https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/whats-safest-way-get-tender-yacht-62030
(which opens with: *"the simple transfer of kit and crew has resulted in people in the water, and
lives being lost"*).

- **Step into the centre, not onto the side.** Verbatim: *"Step down onto the seat rather than the
  tubes"* — weight central, and a seat is a secure foothold where a tube rolls away under you.
- **Keep low, and keep your weight centred** the whole time you are moving in the dinghy. Never
  stand up in a small tender.
- **One person at a time.** Transfers should be *"quick and clean, transferring completely from
  the boat to the tender"* — one person committed and settled before the next moves.
- **Hold on to something above your centre of gravity.** The tender-to-yacht article is specific:
  the good handholds are the **shrouds** or the backstay, not a low stanchion, because a handhold
  below your waist levers you outboard rather than steadying you. Board at a guardrail gate (so
  you can go through crouched) or at the shrouds.
- **Help by offering a shoulder, not a hand.** Verbatim: *"Offer your shoulder, rather than a hand,
  as a support"* — you stay braced and holding on; a hand offered pulls you both over.
- **Secure the tender bow and stern before anyone moves.** *"Use bow and stern lines to secure the
  dinghy alongside – much easier to get on and off"*
  (https://www.safe-skipper.com/dinghies-and-tenders/). For an inflatable, the tender-to-yacht
  article says make the painter fast to the **outside grabrail**, so that if someone's weight goes
  inboard of the centreline the painter holds the near side down and resists capsize.
- **Pass gear separately.** Get a crew member on deck first and hand bags up under the lower
  lifeline; heavy items go up on a halyard or a block and tackle. Nobody climbs with their hands
  full.
- **Look for wash before you start.** Check for passing traffic — a wake arriving mid-transfer is
  how people end up in the water.
- **Do not do it at all if it is marginal.** Same source: if conditions are bad, go to the nearest
  pontoon instead.

## 2.3 The painter

The painter is the tender's bow line. Three separate points, all card-worthy:

- **It must never get near the propeller.** From
  https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/use-tender-safely-60768: keep the painter clear of
  the prop when under way. The classic incident, described on the YBW thread
  https://forums.ybw.com/threads/securing-a-tender-to-the-boat.609979/, is a **grounding**: the
  crew slam the yacht's engine astern to get off, forget the tender is on a long tow, and wrap the
  painter round the prop — turning a soft grounding into a disabled boat. **Shorten or recover the
  tow before any manoeuvring, and always before going astern.**
- **Long enough to be useful.** A long painter lets you lie off other tenders on a crowded dinghy
  pontoon and lets you make the tender fast bow *and* stern alongside for boarding.
- **A person is a better painter than a cleat.** Verbatim, from
  handling-rowing-a-dinghy: *"A human being is the best painter as they'll dampen the motion"* — a
  crew member holding the line absorbs the snatch that a made-fast line transmits.
- **Do not leave it trailing in the water** when you make fast — the loose bight is what finds the
  propeller, yours or someone else's.

## 2.4 Lifejackets in the tender

From https://www.safe-skipper.com/dinghies-and-tenders/, verbatim: *"Wear lifejackets, even for
short trips."*
From https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/handling-rowing-a-dinghy-a-beginners-guide-88389:
*"Always wear a lifejacket with whistle and light"*, and *"avoid dinghying alone; incidents escalate
quickly without assistance."* Also: **never operate a tender while impaired** — the tender trip
back from the pub at night is the single most-cited fatal scenario in the sources.

The RNLI's framing (reported at
https://www.boatsandoutboards.co.uk/resources/safety-at-sea-advice-from-the-rnli/): *wearing a
lifejacket is the single biggest thing you can do to protect yourself*, and this applies **in the
tender and going to and from the vessel**, not only on the yacht. The RNLI runs "lifejacket
locker" schemes at popular destinations precisely because people take lifejackets off to go ashore
(https://rnli.org/news-and-media/2017/june/28/rnli-lifejacket-locker-safety-initiative-helps-take-the-stress-out-of-safety
— *page identified through search; the RNLI site returns 403 to automated fetch, so this is the
initiative's existence, not a verbatim citation*).

One nuance worth knowing from handling-rowing-a-dinghy: in swell, **manually inflated** lifejackets
are more reliable in a tender than automatics — an automatic can fire from spray and leave you
wearing an inflated jacket in a small boat you now cannot move around.

## 2.5 The kill cord — the most important habit with an outboard

**What it is and what it does.** From https://www.rya.org.uk/water-safety/safety-equipment/kill-cord/,
verbatim:

> *"A kill cord is a coiled red lanyard fitted with a quick release mechanism. When used correctly,
> a kill cord will stop a boat's engine if the driver becomes dislodged from the helm position."*

**How it is worn.** RYA, verbatim:
- On a powerboat, **secure the cord around your leg**. It *"should always be clipped back onto
  itself."*
- On a personal watercraft, fasten it *"around the webbing strap of the personal buoyancy."*
- It must not interfere with the steering or throttle controls.

From https://www.mby.com/gear/kill-cords-43376 and https://chieftain.training/kill-cords/, the
reasons for **leg and not wrist**: worn on the wrist it *"can become entangled around the wheel"*
and *"can slip off"*. MBY also warns specifically against clipping it to **clothing or a
lifejacket**, because those can come off the driver, leaving the cord attached to a garment rather
than a person.

**When it must be attached.** RYA: it *"must be worn by the driver whenever the engine is
running."*
MAIB Safety Bulletin SB1/2013 (https://www.gov.uk/maib-reports/safety-warning-issued-after-the-ejection-of-family-of-6-from-rigid-inflatable-boat-in-the-camel-estuary-cornwall-england-results-in-2-people-seriously-injured-and-loss-of-2-lives),
verbatim: *"always attach the cord securely to the driver, ideally before the engine is started,
but certainly before the boat is put in gear."*

**The standard test.** RYA, verbatim: *"Always test your kill cord at the start of each day or
session. Do this by starting the engine and pulling the kill cord to make sure it cuts the
engine."*
MAIB: *"test them regularly to ensure that the engine stops when the kill cord mechanism is
operated"* and *"make sure that the cord is in good condition."*
https://chieftain.training/kill-cords/ gives a fuller three-part pre-departure check:
1. inspect the cord for wear or damage;
2. try to start the engine **without** the cord attached — it should not start;
3. attach it and pull — the engine should stop immediately.

**Changing drivers.** RYA, verbatim: *"If you need to leave the command position, or change driver,
always turn the engine off. The engine should only be re-started when the kill cord has been
secured to a new driver."*
MAIB: *"stop the engine before transferring the kill cord to another driver."*
Two independent authorities, identical instruction — this is a strong card.

**The spare.** RYA, verbatim: *"A second kill cord should always be kept onboard to re-start a boat
if both the driver and their cord fall overboard."* Chieftain adds the crew-facing point: the crew
must **know where it is**, because they are the ones who will need it to restart the engine and
recover the person in the water.

**Why it is the single most important habit — the failure it prevents.** Without a kill cord, a
driver thrown out leaves a **runaway boat**, which typically **circles** under the torque of the
outboard and comes back through the people in the water. That is exactly what happened in the
Camel Estuary, Padstow, on 5 May 2013: a family of six was ejected from an 8m RIB, **nobody on
board was wearing the kill cord**, two died and two were seriously injured. MAIB issued Safety
Bulletin SB1/2013 immediately, warning of a *culture* in which a significant proportion of drivers
do not attach the kill cord when they take the helm.
MBY records the MAIB's figures for 2005–2013: **seven fatalities** from people thrown overboard
without a kill cord being worn, plus 11 injuries and 19 further out-of-control-vessel incidents.

**Care and maintenance.** RYA: do not extend the manufacturer's cord; buy quality lanyards with a
reinforcing cord; do not leave them exposed to the weather; check for wear, **rust and reduced
elasticity**. MBY: extremes of temperature and UV *"may harm the lanyard."*

> **Worth knowing, and a fair "nuance" note rather than a card:** the RNLI does **not** fit kill
> cords on its D-class inshore lifeboats, because in surf a crew member's movement could cut the
> engine at the worst moment; instead the throttle is sprung so it returns to idle. This is a
> designed-in alternative for a specific operating environment, not an argument against kill cords
> on a yacht tender. (Reported at
> https://uk.boats.com/how-to/kill-cord-reminder-rnli-video-of-runaway-powerboat/ — *RNLI's own
> page 403s*.)

## 2.6 Rowing a dinghy

The syllabus requires the crew member to *"handle a dinghy under oars"*, so this is a **DO** item
and the cards should be about the things that go wrong.

From https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/handling-rowing-a-dinghy-a-beginners-guide-88389
and https://www.safe-skipper.com/dinghies-and-tenders/:

- **You row facing aft**, so the boat goes the way your back is pointing. You must **look over your
  shoulder** regularly — this is the single most common failure and the reason rowing tenders hit
  moored boats.
- **Sit centrally on the thwart**, feet braced, weight low.
- Both oars **in the rowlocks and secured** before you push off. Carry the rowlocks with the oars,
  or on lanyards — a rowlock dropped over the side ends the trip.
- **To turn:** pull on one oar and back (push) on the other; the dinghy spins almost on the spot.
  To turn gently, just pull harder on one side.
- **Feathering** — rotating the blade horizontal on the recovery — reduces windage. It matters in a
  breeze and is the difference between making ground and not.
- **Wind and tide beat you before you notice.** Row **out** against the wind or tide so the tired
  return leg is with it, not against it. Judge progress against something ashore, not against the
  water.
- **Oars are the backup, not the afterthought.** Both YM and Safe Skipper list oars (and *"well
  designed oar rowlocks"*) as required equipment even in a tender with an outboard, because the
  outboard is the thing that fails.

## 2.7 Tender safety kit

Compiled from https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/use-tender-safely-60768,
https://www.safe-skipper.com/dinghies-and-tenders/ and
https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/handling-rowing-a-dinghy-a-beginners-guide-88389:

- **Lifejackets** for everyone, worn, with **whistle and light**
- **Kill cord** worn, plus a **spare kill cord** on board in a known place
- **Oars and rowlocks** — the independent means of propulsion
- **A bailer** (and a pump for an inflatable)
- **An anchor and line** for a tender used any distance — it stops you being set away if the engine
  dies
- **A torch**, waterproof and powerful
- **A means of calling for help** — handheld VHF or a phone in a waterproof case
- **Fuel checked before departure** (Safe Skipper lists "fuel verification before departure"
  explicitly), plus a reserve
- **A painter long enough** to make fast bow and stern
- **A lanyard on the outboard** itself — Safe Skipper, verbatim: *"Attach a lanyard to prevent the
  outboard going over the side"*
- **Tell someone on the yacht where you are going and when you expect to be back**

## 2.8 At night

- **A torch is not optional at night** and is the item most often forgotten. From
  use-tender-safely, its three jobs: it lets you operate the tender, it lets you **find your own
  yacht among the moorings** in the dark, and it works as an **emergency navigation light** — you
  show it in time to prevent collision.
- The rafting-up sources make the same point from the other side: coming back to a raft in the dark
  is when people trip over spinnaker poles and open hatches.
- Night, cold water and alcohol are the combination in the majority of tender fatalities. The
  material for a card is: **at night, wear the lifejacket, carry the torch, do not go alone, and do
  not drive impaired.**

# 3. `cc-manners` — Manners and customs

The syllabus bar: *"Understands accepted practice with regard to: use of burgees and ensigns,
prevention of unnecessary noise or disturbance in harbour including courtesies to other craft
berthed alongside. Aware of the responsibility of the boating community to protect the
environment."*

## 3.1 The ensign

**What it is and where it goes.** From https://www.rya.org.uk/regulations/flag-etiquette/, verbatim:

> *"The Ensign shows the country of registry of the vessel and indicates its nationality."*

It occupies *"the most senior position for a flag on a vessel … as close to the stern of the vessel
as possible"* — an ensign staff at the stern, or on the backstay. **(UK-specific)** A UK vessel
wears *"the national maritime flag, the Red Ensign, unless entitled to wear a special Ensign."*
Blue and white ensigns and defaced ensigns are worn only under a **warrant** issued to members of
particular clubs, and only while the qualifying conditions are met.

The Sappers' page adds the traditional exception:
https://www.sappersailing.org/offshore/flag-etiquette — *"Particularly when at sea, yawls and
ketches may fly their ensigns at the top of the mizzen mast."*

**Terminology card:** an ensign is **worn**, not flown. PBO
(https://www.pbo.co.uk/expert-advice/boat-flag-etiquette-ensigns-burgees-71131) makes the point
explicitly.

**When it must be worn.** (UK-specific) RYA: the Merchant Shipping Act requires the ensign to be
worn *"when entering or leaving a foreign port and on demand"*, and the RYA recommends it be worn
*"at all times in daylight, especially when near to or in sight of land or another vessel."*

**Ensign hours — sources genuinely disagree, and the RYA is silent.**

> This is the one Konrad flagged, and the disagreement is real.
>
> - **https://www.rya.org.uk/regulations/flag-etiquette/ — the RYA's own page gives NO times at
>   all.** I asked the page directly whether it contains 0800, 0900, sunrise or sunset and it
>   does not. Its only timing statement is the recommendation to wear the ensign *"at all times in
>   daylight."* So "the RYA says 0800 to sunset" is **not** something you can cite to the RYA.
> - **https://rnyc.org.uk/sailing/flag-etiquette/** (Royal Northumberland YC), verbatim:
>   *"Colours are raised at sea (unless racing), and in harbour from the hours of 0800 (0900 in the
>   winter months from 1 November to 14 February inclusive). Colours are struck (lowered) at sunset
>   or 2100 if earlier."*
>   → gives the **exact winter dates: 1 November to 14 February inclusive**.
> - **https://www.sappersailing.org/offshore/flag-etiquette** (Royal Engineer YC), verbatim:
>   *"In harbour, the ensign should be raised at Sunrise (0800 local time or 0900 1 Nov 14 Feb) and
>   lowered at Sunset (or 2100 local time even if the sun is still up)."*
>   → same times, same dates, but note the **wording differs**: RNYC has "sunset **or 2100 if
>   earlier**", REYC has "sunset, or 2100 **even if the sun is still up**". These describe the same
>   rule — lower at sunset unless sunset is later than 2100, in which case lower at 2100 — but the
>   phrasings are easy to misread as contradictory. Write the card as *"at sunset, or 2100,
>   whichever is earlier."*
> - **https://www.pbo.co.uk/expert-advice/boat-flag-etiquette-ensigns-burgees-71131** gives the same
>   0800/0900 and "sunset or 2100" numbers but explicitly says the daily ceremony is *"not
>   compulsory"* and *"has all but disappeared"* in modern yachting practice.
>
> **How to write it:** the numbers **0800 (0900 from 1 November to 14 February), struck at sunset
> or 2100 whichever is earlier** are consistent across the club sources and are the answer the
> examiner expects — but the card should say these are **naval and club tradition**, observed
> ashore at clubs and by smart yachts, and that the RYA itself does not publish times and much of
> the fleet no longer keeps them.

**One more piece of etiquette worth a card:** ensigns are struck at night at anchor or in harbour,
not left up. PBO's practical advice is to take the staff and ensign out and stow it, rather than
*"wrapping the ensign round the staff and leaving it in situ"*, which chafes and fades it.

## 3.2 Dipping the ensign

From https://rnyc.org.uk/sailing/flag-etiquette/, verbatim:

> *"A flag salute consists of dipping the ensign, by lowering it by one third of the ensign staff.
> If that is impractical hold the ensign staff in a horizontal position. The dip should be
> maintained until the other vessel responds by dipping its ensign, when both vessels should hoist
> their ensigns close up."*

From https://www.sappersailing.org/offshore/flag-etiquette, verbatim:

> *"Members should salute warships of any nationality … by dipping their ensign as they pass. When
> saluting, the ensign should be lowered 2/3 of the way down its staff and not re-hoisted until the
> vessel being saluted has acknowledged the salute."*

> **Disagreement, flagged:** RNYC says lower it **by one third** (flag ends up two-thirds up);
> REYC says lower it **2/3 of the way down** (flag ends up one-third up). The *sequence* is agreed
> and is the examinable bit — **the smaller vessel dips first; the warship acknowledges by dipping
> and re-hoisting; only then do you re-hoist.** The exact fraction is not agreed, so write the card
> around "lower it part-way down the staff" and the sequence, not around a fraction.

PBO's version of the same courtesy is looser and worth noting as modern practice: *"it would be
remiss of you not to lower your ensign at the same time as a nearby warship"* — i.e. if the ship
next to you strikes colours, you strike yours.

## 3.3 The burgee

**What it is.** A club burgee is a triangular flag showing which **club** the owner or skipper
belongs to. RNYC: *"A RNYC burgee signifies that the owner or skipper is a member of the Club.
It (or a Flag Officer's broad pennant) should be worn with the ensign."*

**Where it goes.** RYA: *"The burgee is flown at the main masthead"* — traditionally the most
senior position on the boat. RYA also: *"Flag etiquette states that only one burgee is flown at a
time"*, though flying several is increasingly common.

Modern practice differs and every source acknowledges it: RNYC says *"it is now common practice to
fly the burgee from the lower starboard crosstrees"*; PBO says the masthead is often occupied by
instruments, so *"the starboard spreader is an acceptable alternative"*. If a courtesy flag needs
the starboard spreader, **the burgee moves to port** (REYC states this explicitly:
*"If the Club Burgee is being flown on the Starboard Spreader, it must be moved to the Port
Spreader when a courtesy flag is flown."*).

**(UK-specific) The special-ensign link.** RYA: the burgee *"must match a special Ensign if one is
worn and it should always be higher than the Ensign."* PBO: if you hold a special ensign permit,
*"the relevant burgee must be flown at the same time."* So a boat wearing a blue ensign under
warrant **must** also wear that club's burgee — they are a pair, not two independent choices.

**Sizing, incidental but concrete:** RYA suggests *"a burgee of 15\" in the fly … should look
appropriate on vessels up to 34ft."*

## 3.4 The courtesy flag

**What it is and where it flies.** RYA: courtesy flags are flown at *"the starboard spreaders"* —
the signal position. RYA is also clear on its status: *"There is no legal requirement to fly a
courtesy flag; it is a courtesy that acknowledges that the vessel will respect the laws and
sovereignty of that country."*

**Which flag.** The **maritime** flag of the country you are visiting — which is not always the
same as its national flag. **(UK-specific and a good card):** RNYC, verbatim: *"The courtesy flag
for visitors to the UK is the **Red Ensign**, no matter which part of the UK you are visiting."*
Not the Union flag; not the Saltire or the Welsh dragon.

**Where it must not go.** PBO: *"at the starboard spreader (never at the masthead)."* And the rule
both RYA and RNYC state: **nothing may be flown above a national courtesy flag on the same
halyard.** RNYC, verbatim: *"It is a breach of flag etiquette to fly any flag above a national
courtesy flag on the same flag halyard."*

**Condition matters.** A tattered, faded or wrongly-sized courtesy flag reads as an insult rather
than a courtesy — this is the practical point behind the etiquette, and it is why the flag is
bought new for the trip.

## 3.5 The Q flag

**What it is.** The plain yellow flag "Q" (Quebec) in the International Code — *"my vessel is
healthy and I request free pratique"*, i.e. I am requesting customs and health clearance. Flown at
the **starboard spreaders**, in the signalling position. RYA, verbatim: *"The starboard spreaders
are used for signalling. This is where both a courtesy flag and the Q flag, as signals, should be
flown."*

**(UK-specific, and a recent change — flag it as such.)**
Since Brexit the UK rule has changed and it now catches **UK-flagged boats coming home**, not just
foreign arrivals. From https://www.gov.uk/guidance/sailing-a-pleasure-craft-that-is-arriving-in-the-uk
(*page last updated 20 July 2026*), verbatim:

> *"Fly the yellow 'Q' flag when you enter UK waters (the 12-mile limit)"*
> *"Make sure the flag can easily be seen"*
> *"do not take it down until you've finished reporting to customs authorities"*
> *"If you do not comply you will be liable to a penalty."*

So: hoist Q on crossing the **12-mile limit** inbound, keep it up until reporting is complete,
and the crew normally stay aboard until the skipper has cleared. **This applies to UK-flagged
vessels returning from outside UK territorial waters as well as to foreign-flagged arrivals** —
which is the part most older books and older club pages get wrong. The Royal Engineer YC page, for
example, still says *"Flag Q should be flown when arriving into or from a foreign port that is
(presently) outside the EU"*, which is pre-Brexit framing.

## 3.6 Noise and disturbance in harbour

**Halyard slap** is the classic offence and the syllabus's *"prevention of unnecessary noise"*
almost certainly means this first. A slack halyard slaps the mast all night and is audible across
a whole marina; on an aluminium mast it is a bell.

**The fix, which is what a crew member is actually asked to do:** tension the halyard **away from
the mast** so it cannot touch it. In practice — take the halyard tail out to the toerail, a
shroud, the pulpit, the pushpit or the boom end and set it up tight; or lead it round the
spreaders; or use a short strop to hold it off. Sources describing these methods:
https://goodoldboat.com/silence-those-halyards/, https://theboatgalley.com/quiet-clanking-halyards/
and https://www.practical-sailor.com/mailport-ps-advisor/putting-the-silent-back-into-your-sailboat
(*all three via search extraction rather than clean direct fetch; the technique is uncontroversial
and identical across them*). Clipping the halyard to the pulpit and leaving it slack does **not**
work — it must be **tensioned**.

**It is not just etiquette — (UK-specific) it is usually in the marina contract.** UK marina
regulations and codes of conduct routinely include both of these, e.g. Marina Developments Ltd's
published regulations (https://www.mdlmarinas.co.uk/_assets/mdl-regulations-and-code-of-conduct.pdf)
and Salterns Marina's (https://salterns.co.uk/marina-regulations-and-code-of-conduct/) (*read via
search extraction*):
- owners must not run *engines, generators, machinery, radios or other apparatus so as to cause any
  noise, dust, pollution or nuisance* to other users;
- *halyards, flags, banners and other items attached to boats must be secured so as not to cause
  any noise, nuisance, annoyance or inconvenience* to other marina users **or local residents**;
- some marinas impose **quiet hours** — commonly a state of general quiet **after 2200 and before
  0800**.
Some marinas will charge an owner if staff repeatedly have to board the boat to silence halyards.

**Generators and engines.** Charging by running the engine or a genset is the second offence, and
the rule is the same: not early, not late, and not for hours. Where a marina publishes quiet hours,
those are the hours. Where it does not, the working convention is the same 2200–0800.

**The rest of the harbour-noise list, all of it card-usable:**
- Voices carry over water far further than people expect, especially at night in a raft.
- Music in the cockpit is heard by every boat around you, not just yours.
- Wind generators and self-steering gear left clattering.
- Slamming hatches and washboards, and running the anchor chain out over the bow roller, early.
- Dinghy outboards through a quiet anchorage at speed.
- Coming back to a raft at night: cross quietly, and do not use the neighbour's boat as a
  handrail-and-conversation stop.

## 3.7 Rafting up — courtesies to other craft berthed alongside

Sources: https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/how-to-raft-up-safely-and-securely-74073
and https://www.pbo.co.uk/seamanship/rafting-up-the-stress-free-way-27782

**Ask first.** YM: *"Always request permission before coming alongside."* Motor past first to see
how the inside boat is secured and to pick your spot. PBO: choose a boat with crew aboard and of a
similar size, and *"a quick 'mind if we come alongside?' will endear you to your fellow raftees."*
Discuss timings — *especially if anyone is leaving early*.

**But refusing is bad form.** Yachting Monthly's mooring guide states the convention plainly: *it
is ungenerous and unseamanlike to refuse to have another boat raft up to you*, provided lines and
fenders are right and the raft is not already too big. YM's rafting piece gives the legitimate
grounds for declining: harbourmasters usually cap raft size (*"usually three boats maximum"*), and
*"if you have established that there is a rule, then you are on strong ground to decline politely a
request from an approaching yacht."*

**Fenders.** YM: fenders go at **toerail height, not pontoon height** — you are fendering against
another yacht's topsides, not a wall — and bunch several at the point of contact. PBO adds: make
fenders fast to the **toerail, not the guardwires**, so they cannot pop out when the boats work in
a swell. YM's signal: **fenders already out on your seaward side say "you may raft up to me."**

**Lines — this is the substantive card.** The arriving boat provides **its own** warps and fenders.
YM: run **four separate lines** —
1. **bow and stern lines to the inside boat**,
2. **springs to the inside boat** — to stop fore-and-aft movement, *and* specifically so the rigs
   stay staggered: *"Use the springs to make sure that the rigs are misaligned so that they can't
   crash together in a chop"*,
3. **your own separate shore lines, fore and aft**, run past the inside boat to the pontoon, wall
   or buoy.

The reason for (3) is the point of the whole thing: **your weight must not hang off the inside
boat's cleats.** YM's phrasing is that separate shore lines *"reduce cleat strain on the inside
vessel."* And YM's mooring guide adds the courtesy: **tell the inside boat when your shore lines
are on**, to reassure them they are not carrying you.

PBO: *"Leave tails on your boat"* — do not dump your rope coils on the neighbour's deck.

**Masts.** PBO: *"Once alongside, look up and if your mast is in line with your neighbour's, move
fore or aft as necessary."* Two boats of similar size lying mast-to-mast will clash spreaders in
any chop.

**Crossing other boats — the single best-known rule in the section.**
YM, verbatim: *"You must always walk across another yacht's foredeck."* Never through someone's
cockpit without an explicit invitation. Stay **forward of the mast**. PBO: watch for hazards —
open hatches, spinnaker poles — *"particularly important when returning in darkness."*
The reason is privacy as much as safety: the cockpit is the living room.

**Leaving from the inside of a raft.** YM and PBO agree on the method:
1. Warn the boats outside you in good time.
2. Have all your lines ready to slip (single up, take turns off cleats).
3. The **outside boats rig new bow and stern lines to each other around your bow** and get their
   own shore lines arranged so they stay attached to the shore while you go.
4. You slip and go out **downtide/downwind** — the *opposite* of a normal departure.
5. The outside boats then **ferryglide back alongside** on their shore lines and re-secure.

The crew-level card is simpler than the manoeuvre: **you do not just cast off and drive out of the
middle of a raft. The raft has to be re-rigged around you first, and the boats outside come back
alongside on their own lines.**

## 3.8 Environment — "the responsibility of the boating community to protect the environment"

**(UK-specific throughout.)** The Green Blue is the **RYA and British Marine joint environment
programme** — the natural authority to cite in a UK deck.

### Holding tanks and sewage

From https://thegreenblue.org.uk/you-and-your-boat/info-and-advice/water-pollution-prevention/blackwater-disposal-and-pump-out-locations/,
verbatim:

- *"Fit a holding tank. It is law in some European countries and required when using inland
  waterways that adjoin coastal waterways in the UK e.g. the Caledonian Canal, Scotland."*
- *"Sewage discharge is prohibited by law on inland waterways in the UK."*
- *"Only use sea toilets in open sea where waste will be quickly diluted and dispersed. Try to
  discharge more than 3 miles off the coastline."*
- *"Avoid discharging in areas of poor tidal flushing, such as marinas, or where there are
  shellfish beds."*
- Systems should comply with **BS EN ISO 8099**.
- Raw sewage causes *"eutrophication through nutrient enrichment of the water, resulting in algae
  blooms that block out sunlight and deplete oxygen levels"*, poses public-health risk, and
  *"adversely affects shellfish beds."*
- The Green Blue maintains an **Environmental Facilities Map** of pump-out locations; the Canal &
  River Trust has an equivalent for inland waters.

**The rule in a form a crew member can hold:** *inland waterways — never. Marinas and enclosed
harbours — never (use the shore facilities). Open sea, well offshore — use the heads. Best practice
everywhere: use the shore toilet when there is one.*

**The legal position, stated honestly because this is the bit that gets over-claimed:**
From https://www.rya.org.uk/boating-abroad/holding-tanks/, verbatim: *"There is no international
convention which requires private pleasure craft to fit a holding tank"* — MARPOL Annex IV bites at
400 GT or more than 15 passengers. The Recreational Craft Directive/Regulations require **new**
craft with a toilet to have **provision for** a black-water holding tank (i.e. the plumbing and deck
fitting), which is not the same as requiring one to be fitted or used. **There is no general UK
prohibition on discharging from a small pleasure craft in coastal waters** — the 3-mile figure
above is Green Blue *best practice*, not UK law. Grey water may be discharged.

> **Recent-change watch — Konrad specifically asked.** What I can evidence:
> - The **UK inland waterways** prohibition and the **Caledonian Canal** holding-tank requirement
>   are current (Green Blue, page opened above).
> - The RYA's country table shows a decade of tightening **abroad**, with dates: Netherlands
>   discharge prohibited in all inland and territorial waters **since January 2009**; France
>   requiring retention or treatment for new vessels **from 2008**; Sweden's total ban on pleasure
>   craft discharge into lakes, internal and territorial waters (12nm) **from April 2015**; Finland
>   and Spain prohibiting untreated discharge inside **12nm**; Denmark **2nm** for older/smaller
>   boats; Norway prohibited within **300m** of shore; Turkey introducing a **Blue Card** waste-
>   monitoring scheme in some areas; Germany requiring retention for post-2003 boats over 11.5m ×
>   3.8m.
> - **What I could NOT verify:** any change to the *UK* rules for coastal discharge from pleasure
>   craft in the last few years. The RYA's holding-tank page does not address the UK position at
>   all, and I found no UK instrument changing it. **Do not write a card asserting a recent UK rule
>   change.** Write the cards on the Green Blue best-practice framing, which is safe, correct and
>   the examinable answer, and note "rules abroad vary and are tightening — check the country you
>   are visiting."

### Rubbish

MARPOL **Annex V** applies to pleasure craft. From https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/environment/pages/garbage-default.aspx,
verbatim:

- *"MARPOL Annex V generally prohibits the discharge of all garbage into the sea, except as
  provided otherwise in regulations 4, 5, and 6"*
- *"Unless expressly provided otherwise, Annex V applies to all ships, which means all ships of any
  type whatsoever operating in the marine environment, from merchant ships to fixed or floating
  platforms to non-commercial ships like pleasure crafts and yachts."*
- **Plastics: total ban, no exceptions.** The page notes plastic *"can float for years"* and harms
  marine life.

Crew-level version: **nothing goes over the side. Bag it, stow it, land it ashore, and separate it
for recycling.** (Very limited food-waste exceptions exist far offshore; they are not worth a
Competent Crew card and the IMO page I opened does not state the distances, so do not put numbers
on it.)

### Fuel and oil

From https://thegreenblue.org.uk/you-and-your-boat/info-and-advice/water-pollution-prevention/oil-fuel/,
verbatim:

- *"Avoid over-filling the fuel tank. Allow room for the fuel inside to expand."*
- Use *"a large enough funnel if you have to refuel onboard"* and *"a fuel collar to put over fuel
  nozzles to absorb fuel 'blow back'."*
- *"Place a drip tray under the engine: this is a requirement on many waterways."*
- *"Check the engine bilge for oil before pumping and use an absorbent sock to remove oil and fuel
  in the bilge."* Fit an in-line bilge filter; dispose of the collected oil at an onshore hazardous
  waste facility.
- **Never use detergent on a spill:** *"Never use a detergent to deal with spills – it will only
  break up and disperse the fuel or oil."*
- Keep a **spill kit** aboard.

The crew-level rules: **stop the engine and put a cloth or collar round the nozzle before you fuel;
do not fill to the brim; never pump an oily bilge over the side; never squirt washing-up liquid at
a slick to make it disappear.** The last of these is the counter-intuitive one worth a card —
detergent does not clean up a spill, it hides it and makes it more toxic and harder to recover.

### Antifouling

From https://thegreenblue.org.uk/you-and-your-boat/info-and-advice/water-pollution-prevention/antifouling/:

- Traditional antifouling paints contain **copper or zinc biocides**; *"many are toxic to aquatic
  life"* and accumulate up the food chain.
- Removal is the concentrated-pollution moment: *"Always place a tarpaulin or groundsheet
  underneath the boat to protect the ground and collect contaminated antifoul debris."*
- **Wet-abrade** to keep dust down; wipe with a damp cloth; use an industrial vacuum linked to the
  scraper. **Avoid paint strippers.**
- *"The water from pressure-washing your boat is contaminated with the heavy metal biocides found
  in many antifoul paints."* Do not power-spray near a drain to open water; use bunding, or a yard
  with a **filtered wash-down system**.
- All contaminated waste is **hazardous waste** and must be disposed of *"carefully and legally at
  an approved disposal location."*
- Consider **non-biocidal** coatings — silicone, vinyl, foul-release.

### Anchoring damage — seagrass / eelgrass

From https://thegreenblue.org.uk/you-and-your-boat/info-and-advice/wildlife-habitat-protection/anchoring-and-mooring-with-care/:

- Seagrass is *"one of the most rapidly declining habitats on earth and scarce in UK"* waters;
  it covers only **0.1–0.2% of the global seabed** but stores **10–15% of global carbon**, and it
  is nursery ground for commercially important fish, stabilises sediment and buffers coasts.
- The damage mechanism is twofold: the anchor and chain **scour and abrade** the bed, thinning and
  fragmenting it; and the disturbance **suspends sediment** which then **smothers** the plants.
  Swinging moorings scour a bare circle over years.
- The primary instruction, verbatim: *"Locate Seagrass areas before choosing an anchorage."*
  The page points to Natural England (England), Natural Resources Wales, NatureScot and DAERA
  (NI) mapping, and offers *"The Green Guide to Anchoring & Moorings"*.
- Where seagrass is present, anchor on **sand or mud instead**, or use an **advanced/eco mooring**
  system designed not to sweep the bottom.

### Wash, speed and disturbing wildlife

From https://thegreenblue.org.uk/how-to-boat-safely-around-wildlife-this-summer/, verbatim:

- Distances: *"remain at least 100m away from marine wildlife in the water and 50m away from
  wildlife on cliffs and rocks"*, and keep further off if several boats are present.
- Signs you are disturbing animals: *"seals sliding quickly into the water"*, *"head bobbing from
  birds"*, a cetacean that *"may slap the water with their flipper"*, *"loud alert noises"* and
  animals *"raising their heads in distress"*. **If they react to you, you are too close — back
  off.**
- Birds have very sensitive hearing and are alarmed by engine noise; following **speed limits**
  reduces noise as well as wash.
- Breeding season: *"Birds can be particularly vulnerable during the breeding season which is
  usually between the months of February and September."*
- The behaviour rule for cetaceans: *"Slow down, Keep Quiet, and Keep a Consistent Path"* — sudden
  course and speed changes read as threat.

**Wash** is the crew-level version of all of this and it belongs in a card: your wake is your
responsibility. It disturbs wildlife, erodes riverbanks and saltmarsh, and throws people about on
moored boats and in tenders. **Slow down past moorings, in anchorages, in narrow channels and past
small craft** — and observe posted harbour speed limits, which in UK harbours are commonly 4, 6 or
8 knots and are enforceable by the harbour authority.

# 4. `cc-seasickness` — Seasickness

Deliberately small — 2 or 3 cards. Note the logbook treats this as a **three-way tickbox**
(*"Working efficiency is unaffected / partially affected / severely affected by seasickness"*), not
a knowledge item. It is an observation the instructor records, not something you pass or fail.

Primary source opened: https://www.pbo.co.uk/seamanship/coping-with-seasickness-43182

**Cause — sensory conflict.** Verbatim: the brain balances using the eyes, the muscles and joints,
and the vestibular system of the inner ear, and *"when there is a mismatch between the information
that the brain is receiving from these sources it results in a feeling of imbalance"*, which
triggers the vomiting centre in the brainstem. The canonical example: below decks your inner ear
feels the boat move while your eyes, fixed on a static cabin, report stillness.

**What makes it worse** (all from the same page):
- being **below decks** — motion is greater away from the boat's centre and there is no horizon
- **reading, or looking at a screen / chart** — visual stillness against felt motion
- **cold and wet clothing**
- **fatigue, hunger and dehydration** — the page notes these *"three vulnerabilities compound each
  other"*
- **anxiety**, and **fumes** (diesel, cooking, bilge)
- and, by direct extension of fatigue/dehydration: a hangover — the sources treat alcohol the night
  before as loading all three risk factors at once

**What actually helps:**
- Before you sail: **rest, eat, hydrate**, and dress warm and dry.
- **Get on deck, in fresh air, and look at the horizon.**
- **Take the helm.** The page: concentration plus horizon-gazing is *"remarkably effective"* —
  helming gives you a task, a focus at the horizon, and a sense of control.
- **Eat small amounts frequently and sip water regularly** — an empty stomach is worse than a full
  one, and dry biscuits, ginger and plain carbohydrate are the traditional choices.
- **Stay engaged** — conversation, a job on deck. Idle staring downwards is the worst posture.

**Medication — the timing is the card.** Verbatim:

> *"It is much more effective to prevent seasickness by taking medication in advance of travelling,
> than to wait for the symptoms to start."*

Once you are being sick, an oral tablet will not stay down. So: **take it before you sail**, at the
interval the packet specifies. Second point, from the wider literature (e.g.
https://mahina.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Seasickness-Avoidance-Treatment-v2.17.pdf — *via
search extraction*): **try any remedy ashore first**, because drowsiness is a common side effect and
a drowsy crew member on watch is its own problem.

**The safety point that matters to the skipper.** Same PBO page:

- **Dehydration is the real danger.** For a severely affected crew member, the risk is not the
  nausea, it is fluid loss. *"Oral rehydration solutions (like Dioralyte) are invaluable — they
  replace lost fluids and essential chemicals better than water alone."*
- **A severely seasick crew member is a safety risk and must be clipped on.** Verbatim from that
  page: *"seasickness impairs judgment and weakens the body, creating safety risks; affected crew
  must remain harnessed and clipped on."* They are apathetic, unsteady, and will lean over the
  side — which is where people go overboard. **Be sick to leeward, sitting down, in a lifejacket
  and harness, clipped on.** Never send a seasick crew member to the leeward rail unclipped, and
  never leave them alone in the cockpit.

Third card if there is room: **tell the skipper early.** Seasickness is not a failure of character
and it is easier to manage before it is severe — the skipper can change the crew's task, alter
course to ease the motion, or get them on the helm.

# 5. `cc-lookout` — Rules of the road at Competent Crew level

The **entire** syllabus requirement is *"is able to keep an efficient lookout at sea."*
3–5 cards. **Explicitly out of scope:** give-way and stand-on rules, IRPCS rule numbers, light
characteristics, day shapes, sound signals, buoyage. Those are Day Skipper.

## 5.1 The wording — IRPCS Rule 5

Verbatim, from https://ecolregs.com/index.php?option=com_k2&view=item&layout=item&id=48&Itemid=505&lang=en
(the EU eColregs training text) and confirmed identically at
https://maritimesafetyinnovationlab.org/2023/05/17/spotlight-topic-standing-a-proper-lookout-on-recreational-boats/:

> **Rule 5 — Look-out.** *"Every vessel shall at all times maintain a proper look-out by sight and
> hearing as well as by all available means appropriate in the prevailing circumstances and
> conditions so as to make a full appraisal of the situation and of the risk of collision."*

The four phrases worth pulling apart on a card, because each carries a duty:
- **"every vessel"** — no exceptions, no size limit; it applies to a yacht as much as a ship
- **"at all times"** — not just when there is traffic, and not just on watch
- **"by sight and hearing as well as by all available means"** — eyes and ears first, then
  binoculars, radar, AIS
- **"so as to make a full appraisal … and of the risk of collision"** — looking is not enough; the
  lookout exists to **assess**, and to report

> *Note on transcription: the sources give slightly different wordings of the end of the rule —
> ecolregs has "of the situation and or the risk of collision" and cultofsea has "by sight and
> hearing as by all available means", both of which are typos in the source pages. The IMO wording
> is "…by sight and hearing as well as by all available means appropriate in the prevailing
> circumstances and conditions so as to make a full appraisal of the situation and of the risk of
> collision." I could not open legislation.gov.uk's Schedule 1 or the MCA's copy — both are dead —
> so this is the wording as reproduced consistently by the training sources, not as read from the
> statute.*

**Rule 5 is unusual and it is worth saying so on a card:** it is the only rule that applies to every
vessel, at all times, in all conditions, with no exceptions and no qualifications — and it is the
rule most often cited in collision investigations.

## 5.2 What a good lookout actually does — scanning

The substance, from
https://maritimesafetyinnovationlab.org/2023/05/17/spotlight-topic-standing-a-proper-lookout-on-recreational-boats/,
https://www.cultofsea.com/colregs/part-b-steering-and-sailing-rules-4-19/rule-5-lookout/ and
https://galvanicworks.com/the-360-degree-lookout-problem-why-slow-boats-face-danger-from-all-directions/
(*the last via search extraction*):

- **Scan the whole horizon, 360°, regularly.** A slow boat can be approached from any direction —
  a yacht doing 5 knots is being overtaken by almost everything.
- **Look under and behind the headsail.** This is the yacht-specific blind sector and it is the one
  the syllabus is really about. A genoa hides a wide arc on the leeward bow — exactly where a
  converging vessel sits. The counter is a physical habit: **on the leeward side, duck down and look
  under the foot of the sail, and do it every few minutes.** The maritimesafety piece gives the
  general form of the failure — a helmsman whose view was blocked by *"the corner post of the
  cockpit cover"* and who never saw the approaching sailboat — and notes a lookout's job is to
  *"occasionally point out contacts that might be closing"* and to challenge the helmsman about what
  is hidden.
- **Look astern.** Ships overtake. By the time a crew stands up and looks back, an overtaking vessel
  may already be **2–3 miles off**, which at combined closing speeds is very little time.
- **Move.** A lookout who sits in one place in the cockpit is looking at one sector. Stand up, cross
  to the other side, look under the sail, look aft.
- **Do not just look at the horizon.** Small craft, kayaks, buoys, pot markers, floating debris and
  swimmers are all close-in and low.
- **Never assume you have been seen.** The maritimesafety page's closing principle: recreational
  boaters *"should never assume they've been spotted by other vessels."*

## 5.3 Constant bearing = risk of collision

This is the one piece of technique that makes the difference between watching and appraising, and
it is squarely within "efficient lookout" — it does not require any give-way rule.

Verbatim, from https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/33/83.07 (the US codification of IRPCS Rule 7,
identical wording to the international rule):

> **7(d)(i)** — risk of collision shall be deemed to exist if *"the compass bearing of an approaching
> vessel does not appreciably change."*
> **7(d)(ii)** — *"[risk] may exist even with appreciable bearing changes when dealing with large
> vessels, tows, or vessels at close range."*
> **7(a)** — *"use all available means appropriate to the prevailing circumstances and conditions to
> determine if risk of collision exists"*, and *"if there is any doubt such risk shall be deemed to
> exist."*

**How a crew member does it without a hand bearing compass** — and this is the card:
Line the other vessel up against something **fixed on your own boat** — a shroud, a stanchion, a
pushpit tube — with your own head kept still. Wait, then look again.
- The other vessel has **moved forward** relative to your shroud → she will pass ahead.
- The other vessel has **moved aft** → she will pass astern.
- The other vessel is **still in exactly the same place** and getting bigger → **you are on a
  collision course. Tell the skipper now.**

The formal version is the same thing with a hand bearing compass: take a compass bearing, wait,
take it again. Unchanged bearing + decreasing range = risk of collision. Confirmed in those terms
at the maritimesafety page: *"a sailboat was closing with a constant bearing and decreasing range –
certainly posing a risk of collision."*

**And the "if in doubt" rule from 7(a) is a good crew-level card in itself:** you do not need to be
sure there is a risk. If you are not sure there **isn't**, you must treat it as though there is —
which for a crew member means **report it**.

## 5.4 How to report a sighting

The convention is the **clock bearing**, and it is relative to the boat, not to north.
From https://eoceanic.com/sailing/tips/35/333/naming_convention_for_describing_objects_relative_to_the_vessel's_heading/:
*"An object appearing directly on the starboard beam being at 3 o'clock."*
12 o'clock is dead ahead (the bow), 3 o'clock is the starboard beam, 6 o'clock is dead astern, 9
o'clock is the port beam. Each hour is 30°.

**The format:** *what it is, where it is, roughly how far.* E.g. *"Ship, two o'clock, about two
miles."* / *"Small yacht, ten o'clock, close."* / *"Ship, dead astern, overtaking."*

The same page gives the two alternative conventions a crew member will hear and should recognise:
- **bow / beam / quarter** — "a ship on the starboard bow", "fishing boat on the port quarter",
  "dead ahead", "dead astern". Six sectors, no numbers, and the one most British skippers actually
  use.
- **points** — *"two points off the starboard bow"*; 32 points to the circle, one point = 11.25°.
  Traditional, still used at sea, worth recognising but not worth a card at CC level.

**The reporting rules that matter, from the Royal Navy lookout tradition** (via
https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2018/october/bluejackets-manual-language-lookouts
and https://www.nautinst.org/resources-page/lessons-for-a-good-lookout.html — *both via search
extraction; the principles are consistent and long-established*):
- Report **immediately** — a contact report should take *"2 to 6 seconds from the time of
  sighting"*, in the fewest possible words. Do not wait until you are sure what it is.
- Report **what you see, not what you conclude**. "Two white lights" is a report; "a big ship going
  left" is a guess.
- Report it **again** if it changes — closing, opening, or **steady** (steady is the one that
  matters).
- **Report everything, including what you think the skipper has already seen.** A duplicate report
  costs nothing; an unreported ship can cost everything.

## 5.5 Big ships

Three facts, all crew-level, all within "efficient lookout", none of them a give-way rule:

- **A large vessel cannot easily manoeuvre or stop.** A big ship may take **over a mile** and
  several minutes to stop, and turning takes similar distances. By the time she can see that you
  are in the way, she cannot do much about it.
- **She may physically not be able to see you.** A large ship with the bridge aft has a **blind
  sector extending several hundred metres — on the largest ships, well over a mile — ahead of her
  bow**, inside which a small craft is invisible both visually and, being small and often
  fibreglass, on radar. Source: https://thechartroom.co/2017/07/17/keep-well-clear-of-large-ships-and-their-blind-spot/
  and https://www.bwsailing.com/cc/2016/06/how-to-avoid-a-tankers-blind-spot/ (*via search
  extraction*). A **radar reflector mounted as high as possible** materially improves the chance of
  being detected — a fibreglass or wooden yacht is a poor radar target.
- **In a narrow channel, she has nowhere to go.** She is constrained by her draught and by the
  dredged water; the small boat is the one with options.

The crew-level conclusion, and the card: **assume the ship has not seen you and cannot avoid you.
Report her early, watch the bearing, and expect the skipper to take avoiding action in good time
and to make the alteration large enough to be obvious.**

## 5.6 At night and in poor visibility

- **Preserve night vision.** No white light on deck, no phone screens, no cabin lights spilling
  into the cockpit. Full dark adaptation takes 20–30 minutes and one white torch destroys it.
- **Look slightly to one side of a faint light.** Off-centre vision is more sensitive in the dark,
  so a faint light is easier to hold by looking just beside it.
- **In fog, listen.** Rule 5 says *"by sight **and hearing**"* — and in fog hearing is the primary
  sense. Stop the engine periodically to listen. Get a lookout **forward**, away from engine noise
  and clear of the sails. (Do not go further than this into sound signals — that is Day Skipper.)
- **In fog and at night the lookout is doubled, not reduced.** More people looking, in more
  directions, reporting more often.
- **Radar and AIS are "all available means", not a replacement for eyes.** Rule 5 requires both.

## Appendix — claims from the brief I could NOT fully verify

Listed so they are not written into cards as if they were sourced.

1. **"The RYA teaches OXO on a cleat."** Widely reported by instructors and forum discussion; I
   could not open an RYA page saying so in the RYA's own words. The *disagreement* about locking
   turns is well evidenced; the attribution to the RYA is not.
2. **"Ensign hours 0800 to sunset, 0900 in winter."** The numbers and the 1 Nov–14 Feb dates are
   corroborated by two independent UK yacht clubs, but **the RYA's own flag etiquette page states no
   times at all**, and PBO says the daily ceremony has largely lapsed. It is club/naval tradition,
   not an RYA rule.
3. **The exact fraction for dipping the ensign.** Two sources, two different fractions (§3.2).
4. **A recent change to UK holding-tank / discharge rules.** I found none. Changes I *can* evidence
   are all overseas (§3.8) plus the long-standing UK inland-waterways prohibition.
5. **Maximum-persons plate wording in the current UK regulations.** The 2017 consolidation serves
   Schedule 1 as scanned images; I read the requirement only through the 1996/2004 versions'
   text extraction and derived sources.
6. **RNLI's own tender and kill-cord pages.** rnli.org returns HTTP 403 to automated fetching. All
   RNLI positions above are cited from third parties reporting RNLI guidance. If you want RNLI as a
   named authority on a card, open the page manually first.
7. **MARPOL Annex V food-waste discharge distances.** The IMO page I opened references the
   exceptions in regulations 4–6 but does not state the distances, so no numbers are given above.
