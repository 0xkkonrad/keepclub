# 13 Helmsmanship and sailing

## Name the points of sail, from head to wind round to dead downwind.

<b>Head to wind</b> &mdash; pointing straight at it, sails flapping, no drive.<br><b>Close hauled</b> &mdash; as near the wind as she will sail.<br><b>Close reach</b> &mdash; a little further off.<br><b>Beam reach</b> &mdash; wind on the side.<br><b>Broad reach</b> &mdash; wind over the back corner.<br><b>Run</b> &mdash; wind from behind. Each of them exists on both tacks, so the same angle has a port and a starboard version.

![diagram](cc-points-of-sail.png)

## How close to the wind will a cruising yacht sail?

About 45&deg;, which leaves a wedge of roughly 90&deg; centred on the wind that she cannot sail in at all &mdash; the <b>no-go zone</b>. Steer into it and the sails just flap. To get somewhere upwind you zig-zag across the wedge on one tack and then the other.

## Why does a sail pull the boat along, even going upwind?

A sail is a wing lying on its side. Wind bending round the curve of it sucks the sail forwards and sideways at the same time. The keel is a big flat blade in the water that will not go sideways, so the sideways part is cancelled and what is left drives the boat forward. Downwind there is nothing clever about it &mdash; the wind is simply pushing.

## What are telltales, and how do you steer to them?

Short ribbons stitched near the front edge of the headsail, one each side. Both streaming straight aft means you are on course and the sail is working. The <b>windward</b> one lifting or dancing means you are too close to the wind: bear away a touch. The <b>leeward</b> one dancing means you are too far off: come up. Steer to the windward one and follow it with small movements.

## The front edge of the sail is fluttering. What is happening?

The sail is <b>luffing</b> &mdash; wind is getting round the wrong side of its leading edge, either because you are steering too close to the wind or because the sheet is too far out. Bear away, or pull the sheet in. A sail left slatting hard is <b>flogging</b>, and flogging wrecks the stitching and shakes the whole rig, so it never gets left.

## The tiller is pulling hard against your hand. What is the boat telling you?

That she is trying to turn up into the wind and you are holding her off it. That pull is called <b>weather helm</b>. Holding it means the rudder is sitting at an angle and dragging like a brake, so you are steering hard and going slower for it. Let some mainsheet out and see whether the tiller goes light. If it does not, say so, because from the helm you feel it before anybody else sees it.

## Which way do you push a tiller to turn the boat to starboard?

To <b>port</b>. A tiller always goes the opposite way to where you want the bow to go; a wheel goes the same way, like a car. The way to hold it in your head is that the stern goes where you push and the bow does the opposite.

## How much should you move the helm?

Hardly at all. A yacht answers slowly, so a beginner pushes, sees nothing happen, pushes further, and then has to haul it all back the other way &mdash; and the boat snakes down the sea for the rest of the watch. Move a little, wait for her to come round, then move again.

## What does your wake tell you about your steering?

Look over the stern. A straight wake means you are holding a course; a snake means you are chasing it and over-correcting.

## The skipper says 'steer one-eight-zero'. What do you do, and what do you say?

Say the number back &mdash; 'one-eight-zero' &mdash; so they know it was heard right. Bring her round until that figure sits under the mark on the compass and hold it. Then report honestly what you are actually managing: 'making about one-seven-five' is useful information.

## How do you hold a compass course without staring at the compass?

Get her settled on the number, then pick something ahead that lines up with the bow &mdash; a headland, a chimney, the corner of a cloud &mdash; and steer at that instead. Glance down every few seconds to check the number has not crept. Clouds move and headlands come abeam, so choose a new mark when the old one stops working. Watching the compass alone means chasing the swinging card, and the boat wanders wider than if you looked up.

## Why is nothing metal put down near the steering compass?

A phone, a torch, a tin, a winch handle or a toolbag left beside the compass makes it read wrong, and a wrong compass is worse than no compass because you go on steering the error perfectly. The shelf and the top of the binnacle &mdash; the compass housing &mdash; stay clear.

## What is different about steering under engine?

There is no sail balancing her, so she wanders more and you correct more often. She only steers while water is moving past the rudder: in neutral, or barely moving, the helm does nothing at all and the wind blows the bow off where it likes. Going astern she steers badly and late, so make every turn earlier and bigger than feels necessary.

## How do you steer in a lumpy sea?

Look at the water ahead rather than the instruments. Take the big ones slightly off the bow so she rides over instead of slamming, and come back to your course afterwards. Running downwind, keep the stern square to the wave behind so she does not slew sideways. A few degrees off course to miss a bad one is fine and nobody will mind.

## Why do you keep your head down when the boat is running?

Because the boom is right out over the side, and one careless steer or one wave under the stern can bring it across the cockpit at head height. That is a <b>gybe</b>, and it arrives fast enough to put somebody over the side. Never sit under the mainsheet when running, and look at the boom before you stand up.

## Somebody is working on the foredeck. What does that mean for you on the helm?

Hold her as steady and as level as you can, and warn them before anything changes: 'tacking in ten', 'big one coming'. No sharp turns, no gybe, no bearing away without telling them first and giving them time to get a hand on something. A movement that is nothing in the cockpit throws a person off their feet up there.
