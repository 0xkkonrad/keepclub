# 07 Emergency equipment

## Red parachute rocket flare, red hand flare, orange smoke, white flare — what is each for? {ref=safety}

## Where are the flares kept, and could you tell them apart in the dark?

Usually in a sealed watertight canister or a labelled locker by the companionway, where they can be grabbed on the way out. Find yours on day one and open it in daylight. A <b>hand flare</b> and an <b>orange smoke</b> are short and fat and are held; a <b>parachute rocket</b> is long and thin and is fired; the <b>white</b> collision flare is the odd one out by feel &mdash; a slim tube like the parachute rocket but half its length &mdash; and is the one you must never confuse with a red.

## You have been handed a flare and told to fire it. What do your hands do?

<b>Read the case.</b> The instructions are printed on every one of them and no two makes fire alike, so read them in daylight rather than at the moment.<br>Gloves on. Back to the wind, standing on the <b>downwind, leeward</b> side.<br>A <b>hand flare</b> or an <b>orange smoke</b> is held out at arm's length over the water, angled away from you, the boat and the rig, and left to burn.<br>Do not look at it, and never let anybody look into the end of a tube.<br>What comes off a burning flare is <b>hot slag</b>: molten residue that drips, and it goes through a deck, a sail bag, a dinghy, a liferaft and you. That is the reason for the glove and the arm over the side.

![diagram](fig:flares@handheld,parachute,smoke)

## Why is a red flare not a firework?

Because a red flare says one thing &mdash; a life is in danger here &mdash; and anyone who sees one is obliged to act on it. One red seen from a beach launches a lifeboat, and can launch a helicopter. You fire one when the skipper says to, and for nothing else: not to celebrate, not to test, not to entertain.

## You need to signal distress and have nothing in your hands. What do you do?

Stand where you can be seen and <b>slowly raise and lower both outstretched arms</b> at your sides, over and over. It is a recognised distress signal, it needs no equipment, and it works because nobody else waves like that. At night, flash a torch at whoever might be looking.

## There is a radio on board. What do you actually need to know about it?

It is a <b>VHF</b> set, and it is how the boat calls other boats, a marina, or the coastguard when help is wanted. <b>Channel 16</b> is the distress and calling channel, and it is what the set is normally left listening to.<br>Sending a distress call is the <b>skipper's</b> job and needs a licence of its own. If you are ever told to make one, somebody will tell you exactly what to say &mdash; write it down and read it out.<br>Otherwise you do not touch the radio unless you are asked to.

## What is the distress beacon on board, and what is your part in it?

A small transmitter: an <b>EPIRB</b> registered to the boat, or a <b>PLB</b> registered to a person and carried in a pocket or on a lifejacket. Switched on, it tells the rescue services who you are and where you are, anywhere in the world. Your part is knowing where it lives, and taking it with you if the boat is ever left.

## The order comes to abandon to the liferaft. What is your part in it?

You go when the skipper says to go, and not before: the yacht is a better liferaft than the liferaft is, for as long as she is floating.<br>Lifejacket on and done up.<br>The <b>grab bag</b> comes out with you.<br>Step <b>up</b> into the raft off the deck if you can and stay dry &mdash; a soaked person in a raft is cold within minutes.<br>Count heads out loud as people arrive.

## The liferaft's painter &mdash; what is it, and why does it go on first?

The painter is the long line out of the raft, and it does two jobs: it holds the raft to the yacht, and it is the lanyard you haul on to fire the gas bottle. So it is made fast to a strong point on the <b>boat</b> before the raft goes over the side, every time, and it is cut only once every single person is aboard.<br>The raft goes over the <b>leeward</b> side, where the hull gives it a lee and it lies alongside for you to step into. A raft is all windage and no draught and drifts downwind faster than the yacht does, so one launched to windward blows straight back onto her and ends up against the topsides or under the hull.
