# 14 General duties

## What is a watch system, and what does a day on board look like?

The boat does not stop at night, so the crew is split into <b>watches</b> &mdash; groups who take the deck in turn while the others sleep. A watch is a shift: commonly two, three or four hours, often shorter through the night so that nobody gets all the cold ones. 'Your two hours' means your turn on deck.<br>So expect to be woken in the dark, to get dressed in a moving cabin, and to be on deck within a few minutes of somebody shaking your shoulder. Sleep when you are off watch even if it is the middle of the afternoon.<br>On a five-day course in daylight there may be no night sailing at all and the day is simply long. Ask on day one what the pattern is, when you eat, and where your bunk is.

## Somebody gives you an order and you do not understand it. What do you say?

<b>'Say again.'</b> Or 'I don't understand' or 'which rope?' &mdash; any of them, straight away and out loud. Asking is the correct thing to do and guessing is not: a wrong rope pulled hard costs a sail, and the person who asked has never yet been the problem.<br>Say what you cannot do, at the time: 'I can't reach it' now is worth far more than a failure in thirty seconds.<br>And people shout on boats. It is distance, wind and a flapping sail, not anger, and it stops the moment the noise does. Do not read a raised voice as a telling off.

## What may go down the heads?

What you have eaten and drunk, and a little of the boat's own paper. <b>Nothing else</b> &mdash; no wipes, no sanitary products, no cotton buds, no kitchen roll, no food, no tea leaves. A yacht's heads is a small hand pump on a narrow pipe, not a house drain, and anything else stops it.

## You have used the heads at sea. What is the drill?

Pump it right through &mdash; a good many more strokes than you think, until the bowl and the whole pipe run clear;<br>put the lever or valve back the way you found it;<br>and leave the seacocks the way you found them too. If it blocks, say so straight away. Nobody comes out to a blocked heads &mdash; somebody on board takes the pump apart, in a seaway, and it gets worse the longer it is left.

## You are asked to make hot drinks under way. How do you do it safely?

One hand for yourself and one for the kettle, always.<br>Half fill it, so it cannot slop.<br>Let the cooker swing on its <b>gimbals</b>, the pivoting mount that keeps it level, and never grab it to steady yourself.<br>Brace against the cooker strap or a bulkhead, not against the pan.<br>Boots and sleeves on &mdash; a scald is the commonest injury below decks.<br>In a rough sea nobody goes below without a reason, and hot drinks may simply have to wait.

## You are told to 'stow everything' before leaving. What does that actually mean below decks?

Everything has a place and goes in it: <b>lockers latched</b>, floorboards down, drawers shut, nothing standing on the chart table, no mugs, phones or books left on a shelf or a bunk. Heavy gear low down and near the middle of the boat. A winch handle left in a winch or a tin left on the galley top is a missile the first time she rolls, and she will roll further than you expect.

## When is a deck job actually finished?

When the deck is clear. Every rope coiled and hung on its cleat or winch, every tail off the sidedeck, sail ties, boat hook and winch handles back where they live, and nothing left where a foot can catch it. A line in a heap goes over the side and round the propeller. A bight lying across the deck trips you at the exact moment the boat rolls.

## You are asked to check the bilge. What are you looking for, and what do you do?

How much water is in it, and whether that is more than there was yesterday. A little is normal on most boats. A bilge that was dry this morning and is wet now gets <b>reported to the skipper straight away</b> &mdash; before you pump anything out. Smell it if you can &mdash; never taste it, because bilges collect diesel and engine oil: salt means it is coming in from outside, fresh means a tank or a hose.

## Why are the washboards in and the hatches shut at sea?

Because a wave that breaks over the deck goes wherever it finds a hole, and the one hole that matters is the companionway. A boat knocked flat with the deck sealed comes back up; a boat knocked flat with the cabin open fills. Shut them behind you, and put the board back even when you are only nipping below for a minute.

## You are on watch at night while the skipper sleeps. What are you actually doing?

Steering the course you were given and holding it;<br>looking all round, including astern and to leeward behind the headsail, and at any light you were told to watch;<br>glancing over the instruments and the sails;<br>and staying awake &mdash; stand up, move about, and never keep the watch sitting below out of the wind. Nothing out there is yours to solve. Call the skipper for a ship that is not going away,<br>a change in the wind,<br>a new noise,<br>a light you cannot name,<br>or feeling ill or cold. Wake them early rather than once you are sure.

## Your watch is over. What do you hand over?

Where the boat is and where she is going, the course you have been steering, and anything the skipper told you to watch for;<br>what shipping is about and which lights you have been keeping an eye on;<br>what the wind and the weather have done in your two hours;<br>anything you changed, and anything odd you noticed;<br>and how far the log is filled in. Stay on deck until the person taking over is awake, dressed, clipped on and has their eyes in &mdash; not until they say they are coming.

## The skipper reads out the hourly log entry. What is your job?

Write down exactly what you are given &mdash; the time, the position off the instruments, the course, the speed, the wind &mdash; in the boxes, in ink, at the time. Do not round the numbers, do not tidy them up, do not decide a line is not worth filling in because nothing happened. If the instruments fail, that column of numbers is the only record of where the boat has been.

## When do you get the fenders and warps ready for an arrival?

<b>Before</b> you get there, while there is still sea room and nobody is shouting. Fenders hung at the height of the pontoon, both sides if you have not been told which side you are going in;<br>bow line, stern line and springs led out through the fairleads, <b>outside</b> the guardrails, ends coiled and to hand;<br>boat hook out of its clips.

## What is the crew's part of the daily engine and battery check?

Before it is started: the <b>raw-water seacock open</b>, and a look in the bilge under the engine for oil or water that was not there yesterday.<br>Once it is running: go and look over the stern &mdash; <b>cooling water should be coming out with the exhaust</b>. If it is not, tell the skipper at once.<br>Batteries: switch off the lights, instruments and fridge nobody is using, and read out the voltage when you are asked for it. You look and you report. Nobody expects you to fix anything.

## What is the discipline about fresh water and rubbish on board?

<b>Water</b> &mdash; the tank is all there is until you are next alongside. Pump rather than run the tap, wash up in a bowl, use sea water for the first wash and for the deck, and tell the skipper when a tank runs dry rather than quietly switching to the last one.<br><b>Rubbish</b> &mdash; bag it and stow it below or lash it down, because a bag left in the cockpit goes over the side in the first gust, and all of it comes ashore to the marina bins.

## What do you pack for a week aboard, and what do you wear on deck?

Everything in a <b>soft bag</b>. There is nowhere on a yacht to put a suitcase, and a rigid one will be left on the pontoon.<br>Wear <b>layers</b>: a thin base layer, a fleece or two, then waterproof trousers and jacket over the top. No jeans and no cotton next to the skin &mdash; both hold water and take a day to dry.<br><b>Deck shoes or boots</b>, and thick socks &mdash; wet feet are the first part of you to get cold.<br>A warm <b>hat</b>, and gloves, including a spare pair of both, because the first pair goes over the side.<br>Sun does more damage over water than on land: high-factor cream, a peaked hat, and <b>sunglasses on a cord</b>.<br>A <b>complete second dry set</b> of everything, in a drybag inside the soft bag.<br>Seasickness tablets bought before you travel, and a torch.
