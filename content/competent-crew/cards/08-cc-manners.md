# 08 Manners and customs

## What is the ensign, where is it worn, and when does it come down?

The flag that shows the boat's <b>country of registry</b>, and it takes the most senior position on board: as near the stern as you can get it, on a staff on the transom or clipped to the backstay when the staff is in the helmsman's way. A UK boat wears the <b>Red Ensign</b>; other ensigns need special permission, held through a particular club.<br>It is up in daylight, particularly near land or in sight of another vessel, and a UK boat must wear it entering or leaving a foreign port. At night it comes in and gets stowed, rather than being left wrapped round the staff to chafe and fade.<br>Note the verb: an ensign is <b>worn</b>. Every other flag on the boat is flown.

## What is a burgee, and where does it fly?

A small triangular flag showing which <b>club</b> the owner or skipper belongs to. Traditionally at the <b>masthead</b>, but the masthead of a modern boat is full of instruments and a wind vane, so it usually flies from the <b>starboard spreader</b> instead &mdash; and it shifts to the <b>port</b> spreader when a courtesy flag needs the starboard one. Only one burgee flies at a time.

## Two flags fly from the starboard spreader. What are they, and who decides when?

The <b>courtesy flag</b> &mdash; the maritime flag of the country you are visiting, with nothing above it on that halyard. No law requires one; it is a courtesy, which is why a faded, frayed or comically small one reads as an insult. Coming the other way, a visitor to any part of the UK flies the <b>Red Ensign</b>, not the Union flag.<br>The plain yellow <b>Q</b> flag &mdash; hoisted arriving from abroad to say the boat is healthy and is asking for customs and health clearance, and left up until the skipper has finished reporting. Nobody goes ashore before that is done.<br>What each country wants differs and changes, so the skipper says which flag goes up and when. Your part is bending it on the halyard the right way up.

## It is 2300 in a full marina. What noise are you responsible for?

<b>Halyard slap</b> before anything else &mdash; a slack halyard tapping an alloy mast is a bell, and it keeps a whole pontoon awake. The fix is to pull the tail <b>away</b> from the mast and set it up hard, out to a shroud, the toerail, the pushpit or the boom end. Clipping it to the pulpit and leaving it loose achieves nothing.<br>After that:<br>charging on the engine or a generator early or late;<br>music in the cockpit, which every boat around you hears as well as you;<br>a wind generator left spinning;<br>hatches and washboards dropped rather than placed;<br>the chain run out over the roller at dawn;<br>a tender driven fast through a quiet anchorage;<br>and <b>voices</b>, which carry over water far further than anyone believes &mdash; in a raft at night you are talking directly into somebody's bunk. Most marinas publish quiet hours, commonly 2200 to 0800, and most put halyards in the berthing conditions.

## Another yacht asks to raft alongside you. What happens?

The answer is almost always yes &mdash; everyone needs a berth and refusing is poor form &mdash; but it is the skipper's call, not yours: the harbourmaster may cap the raft, often at three, and a yacht far heavier than you in a blow is a real reason to say no.<br>Fenders already out along your outside are the signal that you may come alongside.<br>Your part starts once she is coming: fenders down at her toerail height, and take her lines.<br>Then somebody settles the one thing that actually matters between the two boats: <b>who is leaving, and at what time</b>. An early start changes what everybody has to rig, and nobody wants to discover it at 0500.

## You are rafting onto another yacht. What goes on, and in what order?

Ask permission first, then:<br>fenders at <b>toerail height</b>, because you are fending off topsides and not a wall, made fast to the toerail rather than the guardwires, and bunched where the two hulls actually touch;<br>bow and stern lines onto her, and <b>springs</b>, so you cannot surge back and forth;<br>then your <b>own</b> bow and stern lines led past her to the pontoon, so that your weight is carried by the shore and not by her cleats, and say so once they are on;<br>shift fore or aft until the two masts are not level, because rigs at the same height will find each other in any chop;<br>and keep your rope tails on your own deck.

## You are on the outside of a raft. How do you get ashore?

Across each boat's <b>foredeck</b>, forward of the mast, one person at a time. <b>Never through a cockpit.</b> A cockpit is somebody's sitting room.<br>Go quietly, and more quietly after dark: no shortcut over the sprayhood, watch for open hatches, poles and low guardwires, and do not stop for a conversation hanging off a stranger's rigging.

## What must never go over the side?

<b>Plastic</b>, absolutely and everywhere: bags, film, packaging, rope ends, cable ties, a cigarette filter.<br><b>Oil</b>, and anything with oil in it &mdash; a filter, an oily rag, the water under the engine.<br><b>Rubbish</b> of any kind. Food scraps are the one exception, and only well offshore.<br>Chemical toilet contents, old antifouling, dead batteries and out-of-date flares all come ashore to somewhere that will take them.<br>Sewage from the heads is the one that is not a flat yes or no: it depends where in the world you are, so ask.

## Where may the heads be emptied?

It depends where you are, so ask &mdash; but the version that is never wrong is: <b>not in a marina, a harbour or any enclosed water</b>, and not on UK inland waterways, where it is prohibited by law.<br>Use the shore toilet where there is one, or the boat's <b>holding tank</b> and a marina pump-out.<br>At sea, well offshore, where it disperses. The Green Blue's advice is to get more than 3 miles out and to stay away from shellfish beds and anywhere with poor tidal flushing &mdash; that is best practice, not UK law.<br>Abroad it very often <b>is</b> law, the limits differ from country to country, and they have been tightening for years. Whoever is planning the trip checks the country you are going to.

## You are helping to refuel and there is diesel on the water. What must you not do?

Reach for the washing-up liquid. Detergent does not clean up a spill &mdash; it breaks it into droplets nobody can recover and pushes it down into the water, so the slick disappears and the damage does not. Use the absorbent pads in the spill kit.<br>Not making the spill is the real job: a big funnel or a collar round the nozzle to catch blow-back, and stop short of the brim, because fuel expands.<br>The same rule covers the boat's other poisons. Oily bilge water goes into a container, not over the side, and antifouling scrapings and wash-down are caught on a sheet ashore, because the coating is toxic on purpose.

## Why does it matter where the anchor goes down?

Because chain sweeps. <b>Seagrass</b> is nursery ground for fish and one of the fastest-disappearing habitats there is, and an anchor and its chain scour and break it up, then raise sediment that settles on whatever survived. A swinging mooring wears a bare circle in it over the years.<br>So sand or mud in preference to weed, and use the <b>eco-moorings</b> where somebody has gone to the trouble of laying them. Finding the seagrass is the skipper's job. Knowing why it is being avoided is yours.
