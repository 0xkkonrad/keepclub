# 10 Tender usage

## What does the tender's plate tell you, and how do you use it?

<b>Most</b> tenders carry a <b>builder's plate</b>, and the two numbers that matter are the <b>maximum number of persons</b> and the <b>maximum load in kilograms</b> &mdash; people, fuel, water, shopping and kit bags.<br>Small tenders under 2.5 m are outside the regulations and often have no plate at all. If there is none, ask the skipper what she is rated for and use <b>freeboard</b> as the test: if the side is close to the water, take somebody or something out.<br>Either way the plate figure is a maximum in calm water on a good day, not a target &mdash; if it is choppy, cold, dark, or any distance, take less and make two trips.

## Why is an overloaded tender the classic way people drown within sight of the marina?

Because the <b>freeboard</b> goes. Freeboard is the height of the side above the water, and it is the entire margin against a wave or a passing boat's wash. Load the dinghy down and one wake comes over the side; it fills, and it loses stability and buoyancy at the same moment. A hard dinghy is worse than an inflatable, whose tubes still float when it is full of water. So keep the weight <b>low and central</b>, nobody perched on the tube or the gunwale, and put bags on the floor rather than on your back &mdash; a rucksack floats for a minute, then floods and holds you under.

## How do you get into a tender?

One person at a time, and nobody moves until the one before is sitting down. The tender is held, or made fast bow and stern, before anyone steps. Step down into the <b>middle</b> of it, onto the floor or a seat, <b>never onto the tube or the gunwale</b> &mdash; it rolls away under your foot and puts you between two hulls. Keep low, and hold something <b>above your waist</b>: the shrouds, not a stanchion, because a low handhold levers you outboard instead of steadying you. Go down with empty hands and have the bags passed after. Look for wash coming before you start, and if it is marginal, do not do it at all.

## Who gets into the tender first, and who last?

Somebody steady goes in <b>first</b> and stays sitting down to hold the dinghy against the boat, take the bags and steady each person as they arrive; the last one aboard is the one holding on until everybody else is settled. Boats and schools order it differently beyond that, so do what your skipper says &mdash; what never changes is that the tender is held the whole time and only one person is moving at once. If you are helping somebody in, offer a <b>shoulder</b> to lean on rather than a hand: a hand pulls you both over.

## Do you really wear a lifejacket for a two-minute row ashore?

Yes, and that short familiar trip is the one people die on &mdash; at night, coming back, often after a drink, with nobody watching. Everyone in the tender wears one, every time, with the whistle and light on it. Do not take it off just because you are going ashore and would rather not carry it. Do not go alone if you can avoid it, and nobody drives a tender who has been drinking. One nuance for a small boat: a <b>manually</b> inflated jacket can be the better choice in a wet dinghy, because an automatic can fire on spray and leave you inflated in a boat you can no longer move about in.

## What are the rules about the painter?

<b>Painter</b> is the general word for a small boat's bow line &mdash; the tender has one and so does the liferaft. The tender's is made fast before anybody gets in, and while you are alongside it is best held in somebody's hand, because a person soaks up the snatch that a hard-made line jerks against. Keep it <b>short and inboard</b>. Never let a bight of it trail in the water: a trailing painter is the thing that goes round a propeller. If the tender is on a tow astern, shorten it in before any close manoeuvring and always before the engine goes astern.

## How do you row a tender?

Sitting on the thwart facing <b>aft</b>, so the dinghy goes the way your back is pointing. Both oars in the rowlocks, and the rowlocks tied on, before you push off &mdash; one dropped over the side ends the trip. Sit central with your feet braced and pull short even strokes; a short tender does not reward a long heave, it just slews. <b>Look over your shoulder every few strokes</b>, because you are facing away from everything you are about to hit. To turn on the spot, pull on one oar and push on the other.

## You are rowing to a landing with a tide running across you. Where do you aim?

<b>Up-tide</b> of it, and further up than feels right, because you go where the tide takes you while you row and a tender does walking pace at best. Judge it against something ashore &mdash; line the landing up with something behind it and watch whether it stays in line &mdash; because the water gives you nothing to measure against. Same rule for wind: row <b>out</b> against it while you are fresh, so the tired leg home is the one being helped. If you are losing ground, get hold of something moored before you are set away, rather than rowing harder.

## What is a kill cord, and what does it prevent?

A red coiled lanyard between the outboard's cut-out switch and the driver. If the driver is thrown away from the helm the cord pulls out and the <b>engine stops</b>. Without it, an outboard left running with nobody steering does not simply drive away &mdash; it turns and <b>circles back</b> through the people it has just thrown into the water. That is exactly what happened in the Camel Estuary in 2013, when six of one family went over the side and the boat circled back into them, killing two.

## Where does the kill cord clip, and where must it never clip?

Round your <b>knee or thigh</b>, clipped back onto itself, where it cannot foul the wheel or the throttle; on a jet ski, to the webbing of the buoyancy aid. Never to the <b>console</b> or a grab rail, which is the same as not having one.<br>Never round a <b>wrist</b> &mdash; it tangles in the wheel and slips off.<br>Never to <b>clothing or a jacket</b> you could come out of, because then the cord stays with the garment and the engine keeps running.

## What is the drill with the kill cord?

<b>Test it at the start of each day</b>: start the engine and pull the cord &mdash; the engine must stop. Look the lanyard over for wear, rust and stiffness while you have it in your hand.<br><b>Attach it to the driver before you start</b>, and certainly before the boat goes into gear.<br><b>Changing driver: stop the engine.</b> It is restarted only once the cord is secured to the new driver, never handed across while it is running.<br>A <b>spare kill cord</b> stays on board in a place the crew know about &mdash; if the driver goes over the side wearing theirs, the spare is the only way anybody restarts the engine to go and get them.

## What goes in the tender before you leave the yacht?

A <b>lifejacket</b> each, worn.<br><b>Oars and rowlocks</b> even with an outboard, because the outboard is the thing that fails.<br>A <b>bailer</b> on a lanyard, and the pump if she is an inflatable.<br>The <b>spare kill cord</b>.<br>A waterproof <b>torch</b>.<br>A <b>way of calling for help</b> &mdash; a handheld radio, or a phone in a waterproof case.<br>Fuel actually checked rather than assumed.<br>A small anchor and line if you are going any distance.

## What is different about the tender at night?

The <b>torch</b> stops being optional and it is the thing everyone forgets. It does three jobs: you can see what you are doing, you can pick your own yacht out of a mooring field of identical white boats, and you can shine it at anything coming towards you &mdash; a dinghy at night is invisible until you make it otherwise. Tell somebody on board <b>where you are going and when you will be back</b>, so that if you do not come back it is noticed while it still matters.
