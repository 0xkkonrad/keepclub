# 09 Rules of the road

## What is a lookout for, and what do you keep it with?

<b>Eyes and ears.</b> Sight first, but hearing counts and is written into the rules for a reason &mdash; in fog or round a headland you often hear an engine, a bell or a fog signal before you see anything. Binoculars, radar and AIS come after that, not instead of it.<br>Every boat keeps one, all the time, not only when there is something about. And the job is not simply to look: it is to work out whether anything out there is getting closer, and to tell the skipper.

## How do you actually scan?

All the way round, over and over, and <b>including astern</b> &mdash; a yacht doing 5 knots is overtaken by nearly everything afloat, and by the time somebody thinks to look behind, an overtaking ship can already be close.<br><b>Stand up and move.</b> Sitting in one corner of the cockpit you are watching one sector of the sea.<br>The blind spot on a yacht is the genoa. It hides a wide arc on the leeward bow, which is exactly where a converging ship sits, so duck down and look <b>under the foot of the sail</b> every few minutes.<br>And look close in as well as at the horizon: pot markers, kayaks, swimmers, a half-submerged log.

## How do you know you are on a collision course, with nothing in your hands?

Line the other vessel up against something fixed on your own boat &mdash; a shroud, a stanchion, the pushpit &mdash; and keep your head still. Wait a minute. Look again.<br>She has crept <b>forward</b> of the shroud: she will pass ahead of you.<br>She has slid <b>aft</b> of it: she will pass astern.<br>She is in exactly the same place and getting bigger: <b>you are on a collision course.</b> Tell the skipper now.<br>You do not have to be certain. If you cannot satisfy yourself that there is no risk, treat it as though there is one and report it.

## You have seen something. How do you tell the skipper?

Straight away, in a handful of words, and without deciding anything.<br><b>What it is, where it is, roughly how far</b> &mdash; 'ship, two o'clock, about a mile'.<br>The clock runs round the boat, not the compass: twelve is dead ahead, three is out to starboard, six is astern, nine is out to port. 'Fishing boat on the port quarter' does the same job if that comes more naturally.<br>Report what you can see, not what you think it is going to do. Report it again if anything changes &mdash; and especially report that it has <b>not</b> changed. And report things you assume the skipper has already seen: saying it twice costs nothing.

## A big ship is coming up the channel. Why does that matter to you on the foredeck?

Because she can do almost nothing about you. A large ship takes over a mile and several minutes to stop, and much the same to turn, so by the time anyone aboard could see a problem it is too late to fix it. In a dredged channel she has no room to go anywhere anyway &mdash; you are the one with options.<br>And she may genuinely not be able to see you: the rules allow a blind sector ahead of her bow of up to <b>500 metres</b> &mdash; a quarter of a mile of sea in which a small yacht is simply not visible from the bridge &mdash; and a small fibreglass yacht is a poor radar target as well.<br>So assume you have not been seen, and tell the skipper the moment you spot her, not when she is close.
