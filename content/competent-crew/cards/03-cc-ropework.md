## You are handed a warp and told to make it fast on a cleat. What is the sequence?

A full <b>turn round the base</b> of the cleat first, taken to the <b>far</b> horn rather than the near one so the rope cannot jam on itself. That turn holds the load while you do the rest.<br>Then <b>figure-of-eight turns</b> across the horns, diagonally over the middle &mdash; three or four of them in modern slippery line, not one.<br>Leave the tail lying fair and free, never buried under a coil, so the next person can let it go in the dark.

## Do you finish a cleat with a locking hitch?

Ask, because practice genuinely differs and you will meet both. A <b>locking hitch</b> &mdash; the last turn twisted so it traps the tail &mdash; is normal on a mooring warp that is going to sit there for a week. It is wrong on anything you may have to release while it is loaded: a halyard, a sheet, or your line on a shared bollard that another crew may need to cast off. It is a deliberate jam, and a jam has to be picked out at the moment you least have time.

## How do you make a line fast to a bollard?

<b>Single bollard</b>, meaning one post: a <b>bowline</b> dropped over the top if the line can be tied before the load comes on, a <b>round turn and two half hitches</b> if it cannot.<br><b>Double bollard</b>, two posts or one with a cross-bar: lead the line in a <b>figure of eight</b> round the two heads, crossing between them each time, and finish with a turn. The crossed turns hold each other down; turns dropped straight over one head lift off the moment the line goes slack.

## Somebody else's mooring line is already on the bollard. Where does yours go?

Not on top of it. Pass the eye of your line <b>up through</b> theirs from underneath, then drop it over the post &mdash; this is <b>dipping the eye</b>. Both eyes sit on the bollard and either boat can lift its own off without disturbing the other. Drop yours straight on top and you have moored the boat that got there first to the quay until you come back.

## You are told to let a warp out under control while the boat moves. How?

With <b>turns on</b>, letting the rope slip round them: a round turn on the cleat, or two or three round a winch drum. The friction holds the boat, not your arms. Lean back, feed it out, add a turn for more grip and take one off for less. To stop it, <b>snub</b> it &mdash; hold the tail hard in against the turns and let the rope pull up short. Keep your hands an arm's length back from the cleat or the drum, and never try to hold a loaded rope on grip alone.

## How do you throw a line to somebody ashore?

Make your own end fast on board first, or stand on it, or the whole rope goes in the water with the throw.<br>Coil it down loosely, then split the coil: about a third in the throwing hand, the rest in the other hand held open so it can run.<br>Shout, wait until they are looking, and throw the small coil <b>over</b> them or just past them rather than at them.<br>Never with a hand through the coil.<br>If you miss, pull it in and coil it again &mdash; a rope left floating goes under the boat and round the propeller. And if you can step ashore, step rather than jump.

## How do you spot a badly led rope before the load comes on?

By looking along the whole run of the line while it is still slack, because afterwards you cannot fix it. A line that turns a corner on the way to its fairlead, chafes on a stanchion, or crosses another line under load is badly led. On a cleat, a turn taken to the near horn first, or a tail trapped under the loaded part, locks solid and will not come off when it is wanted. A rope that will not <b>render</b> when you ease it is telling you the same thing. Take the load off it and start again.

## Why do you never put a hand or a finger inside a loop of rope?

Because a bight closes, and it closes faster than you can pull out of it. A loop lying loose round your hand or your foot becomes a noose the instant the rope comes tight, and people lose fingers this way every season. So no hand through a coil you are carrying or throwing,<br>no rope wrapped round a hand or a wrist for a better grip,<br>no standing inside the bight of a warp that is running out,<br>and stand outside the angle of a line under strain, never in it.

## You are given a length of split hose to put on a warp. Where does it go?

On the <b>rope</b>, at the exact point that touches the fitting, taped there so it cannot slide off as the boat moves about all night. Padding the fairlead or the toe rail instead achieves nothing &mdash; it is the rope that is being sawn through, so it is the rope that gets the jacket.

## The line is made fast. What happens to the rest of the rope?

It gets made up. Coil the tail, reach through the coil and pull a bight of the loaded part through, twist it, and drop that loop over the cleat horn &mdash; the coil hangs off the loaded part, so it can be shaken free and let run without lifting it off first. Nothing is left in a heap on the sidedeck and nothing hangs over the side: a tail in the water finds a propeller, and it does not have to be yours. Ropes then live coiled, dry and out of the sun, and nobody stands on them.
