# 04 Fire precautions and firefighting

## What three things does a fire need?

Fuel, air and heat &mdash; the <b>fire triangle</b>. Take away any one of them and the fire goes out. That is all any firefighting on a boat is doing: a fire blanket takes away the air, water takes away the heat, and shutting off the gas or the fuel line takes away the fuel.

## Where do fires actually start on a yacht?

The <b>galley</b> &mdash; a pan of fat, or a tea towel left over a lit ring.<br>The <b>gas</b> &mdash; a perished hose, or a ring turned on and not lit.<br>The <b>engine bay</b> &mdash; diesel or oil dripping onto a hot exhaust.<br>The <b>electrics</b> &mdash; chafed wiring behind a panel, a loose battery terminal, a charger left running.<br><b>Fuelling</b> &mdash; petrol vapour off a spilt outboard can.

## Why does a gas leak on a boat end up in the bilge?

Cooking gas is <b>heavier than air</b>. In a house it would drift up and out of a window; on a boat it runs downhill, through the cabin sole, into the bilge &mdash; the lowest and least ventilated space you have. It does not blow away. It sits there and waits for a spark.

## You smell gas below decks. What must you not touch?

Anything electrical. Do not switch something on <i>or off</i>, do not start the engine, and <b>do not run the bilge pump</b> &mdash; every switch makes a small spark, and the bilge is exactly where the gas has gone. Turn the gas off at the bottle, open the hatches and washboards, get everyone on deck, tell the skipper.

## What is the routine every time you finish at the cooker?

Turn the gas off <b>at the bottle first</b> and leave the burner alight until the flame dies on its own, then turn the ring off. That empties the hose, so a joint that weeps overnight has nothing left to leak. Turning the ring off first leaves the whole line sitting there charged.

## Why never throw water at an electrical fire?

Water conducts, so you and the bucket become part of the circuit. Kill the power first &mdash; at the breaker if you know which one, at the <b>battery isolator</b> if you do not &mdash; and then use the dry powder extinguisher.

## What should you find on your boat before you sail, and where will it be?

The <b>extinguishers</b>, usually one at the companionway and one forward &mdash; sited so there is always one between you and a way out, never one you would have to reach across the fire to get.<br>The <b>fire blanket</b>, in the galley within arm's reach of the cooker but not hung directly over it.<br>Take each one off its bracket once in daylight, so your hands know how it comes off in the dark.

## The engine bay is alight. What is the one thing you must not do?

Do not lift the engine box or the companionway steps. It is short of air in there; open it and you hand the fire a lungful, and it comes out at you. Stop the engine, shut off the fuel and the gas, leave the space shut, and shout for the skipper.

## What are the rules about smoking on board?

On deck only, never below, and never near the gas locker or while a fuel filler cap is off. Sit on the <b>leeward</b> side so ash and sparks blow over the side rather than down the coachroof, and put the butt out in a tin &mdash; not over the side and not into a cockpit locker.

## You find a fire. What do you do, in order?

<b>Shout</b> &mdash; the word 'fire', loud enough for the whole boat, and say where it is.<br><b>Lifejackets on</b>.<br><b>Everyone on deck</b>, and upwind of the smoke.<br>Pass up the nearest extinguisher or the blanket.<br>Count heads.<br>Then do what the skipper tells you. Whether to fight it is the skipper's call, and smoke below decks will have you down long before the flames get to you.
