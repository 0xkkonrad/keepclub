# 03 The core sentence

## What is the basic word order in a Toki Pona sentence?

Subject, then predicate, then object &mdash; the same SVO order English uses. <b>jan li moku e kili</b> breaks down as "person" + [predicate marker] "eats" + [object marker] "fruit": <i>the person eats fruit.</i>

## What does the particle "li" do?

It marks the start of the predicate &mdash; everything from <b>li</b> onward is what the subject does or is. <b>jan li pona</b> is "the person is good"; <b>soweli li moku</b> is "the animal eats." Without <b>li</b>, a listener can't tell where the subject ends and the action begins.

## When does a sentence NOT need "li"?

When the subject is exactly <b>mi</b> (I/we) or <b>sina</b> (you) on its own, with nothing else describing it. <b>mi moku</b> is "I eat," not <b>mi li moku</b>. Add anything to the subject &mdash; a second noun, a modifier &mdash; and <b>li</b> comes back: <b>mi mute li moku</b>, "we (many) eat."

## What does the particle "e" do?

It marks the direct object, the thing the action is done to. In <b>mi moku e kili</b>, <b>e</b> shows that <b>kili</b> (fruit) is what gets eaten, not a second subject. Without <b>e</b>, a string of nouns after the verb would just pile up with no way to tell object from description.

## How does Toki Pona show tense &mdash; past, present, or future?

It mostly doesn't grammatically; the same sentence can describe past, present, or future action, and context (or an added time word like <b>tenpo pini</b>, "past time") carries the difference when it matters. Verbs never conjugate.

## How does Toki Pona show whether a noun is singular or plural?

It generally doesn't mark this either &mdash; <b>soweli</b> can mean "animal" or "animals" depending on context. Where the count genuinely matters, a number or a word like <b>mute</b> ("many") is added instead of an ending.

## How can the same word be a noun, a verb, and a describing word?

Toki Pona doesn't sort words into fixed parts of speech; a word's role comes entirely from its position in the sentence. <b>moku</b> is "food" as a noun, "to eat" as a verb, and "edible/food-related" as a modifier &mdash; the grammar around it, not the word itself, decides which.
