# 02 Sounds and spelling

## How many vowels does Toki Pona have, and how are they pronounced?

Five: <b>a e i o u</b>, pronounced roughly as in Spanish or Italian &mdash; <b>a</b> as in "father," <b>e</b> as in "bed," <b>i</b> as in "machine," <b>o</b> as in "more," <b>u</b> as in "food." These are five vowel categories rather than five rigid accent-free sounds, so speakers' exact pronunciation can vary. Standard spelling has no silent letters.

## How many consonants does Toki Pona have?

Nine: <b>j k l m n p s t w</b>. <b>j</b> is pronounced like English "y" (as in "yes"), and <b>w</b> like English "w." The rest sound close to their English values. Letters like b, c, d, f, g, h, q, r, v, x, y, and z do not appear in standard Toki Pona spelling.

## What is the basic shape of a Toki Pona syllable?

The first syllable has an optional consonant, then a vowel, then an optional <b>n</b>: (C)V(n). Every later syllable must begin with a consonant: CV(n). <b>a</b>, <b>pi</b>, <b>tan</b>, and <b>mun</b> are all complete, valid syllables on their own; a word is just one or more valid syllables joined together, like <b>to-ki</b> or <b>mu-si</b>.

## Which syllable is stressed in a Toki Pona word?

Always the first one. <b>PO-na</b>, not po-NA; <b>TO-ki</b>, not to-KI. The rule never has exceptions, which makes an unfamiliar word easy to pronounce correctly on sight.

## Are consonant clusters allowed within a word?

Two consonants can sit side by side across a syllable boundary when the first is a final <b>n</b>, as in <b>an-pa</b>, <b>ten-po</b>, and <b>lin-ja</b>. Within a syllable, there is at most one opening consonant and the only possible final consonant is <b>n</b>. The sequences <b>nn</b> and <b>nm</b> are avoided, as are the syllables <b>ji</b>, <b>ti</b>, <b>wo</b>, and <b>wu</b>. Before <b>p</b>, a written <b>n</b> may sound like <b>m</b>, but it is still spelled <b>n</b>.

## How are foreign names adapted into Toki Pona's sounds?

By adapting their pronunciation, not simply their spelling, to Toki Pona's sounds and valid syllables. Unavailable sounds are approximated, consonant clusters are reshaped, and every noninitial syllable receives an opening consonant. "Canada" is commonly adapted as <b>Kanata</b>; other names may have several reasonable forms because speakers prioritize recognizability differently. An adaptation must still avoid <b>ji</b>, <b>ti</b>, <b>wo</b>, <b>wu</b>, <b>nn</b>, and <b>nm</b>.

## Why is "Kalauto" not a valid Toki Pona name?

It divides as <b>ka-la-u-to</b>, leaving the noninitial syllable <b>u</b> without an opening consonant. Only a word's first syllable may begin with a vowel, so a speaker would need to choose a different adaptation.

## Which spelling follows Toki Pona phonotactics: "tenpo" or "timo"?

<b>tenpo</b>. It divides into the valid syllables <b>ten-po</b>; the adjacent <b>np</b> lies across that boundary. <b>timo</b> begins with the forbidden syllable <b>ti</b>.
