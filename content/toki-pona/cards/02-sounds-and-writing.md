# 02 Sounds and spelling

## How many vowels does Toki Pona have, and how are they pronounced?

Five: <b>a e i o u</b>, pronounced roughly as in Spanish or Italian &mdash; <b>a</b> as in "father," <b>e</b> as in "bed," <b>i</b> as in "machine," <b>o</b> as in "more," <b>u</b> as in "food." No silent letters and no vowel sounds beyond these five.

## How many consonants does Toki Pona have?

Nine: <b>j k l m n p s t w</b>. <b>j</b> is pronounced like English "y" (as in "yes"), and <b>w</b> like English "w." The rest sound close to their English values. Letters like b, c, d, f, g, h, q, r, v, x, y, and z never appear.

## What is the basic shape of a Toki Pona syllable?

An optional consonant, then a vowel, then an optional <b>n</b>: (C)V(n). <b>a</b>, <b>pi</b>, <b>tan</b>, and <b>mun</b> are all complete, valid syllables on their own; a word is just one or more of these joined together, like <b>to-ki</b> or <b>mu-si</b>.

## Which syllable is stressed in a Toki Pona word?

Always the first one. <b>PO-na</b>, not po-NA; <b>TO-ki</b>, not to-KI. The rule never has exceptions, which makes an unfamiliar word easy to pronounce correctly on sight.

## Are consonant clusters allowed within a word?

No two consonants ever sit side by side, and a syllable's only possible final consonant is <b>n</b> &mdash; never before another <b>n</b>, and never <b>m</b> before <b>p</b> in writing (it's spoken as if it were, but spelled with <b>n</b>). This is why the sound set stays easy to pronounce for speakers of very different native languages.

## How are foreign names adapted into Toki Pona's sounds?

By reshaping them to fit the syllable pattern and the available sounds &mdash; consonants outside the nine are swapped for the nearest match, and clusters get vowels inserted to break them up. "Boston" becomes <b>Posina</b>, and "Claude" becomes <b>Kalauto</b> or similar; there's no single official transliteration, so spellings vary between speakers and communities.
