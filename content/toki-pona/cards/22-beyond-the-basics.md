# 22 Beyond the basics

## What does "a" mean?

An interjection expressing emphasis, emotion, or confirmation &mdash; like "ah," "oh," "wow," "ha," or an acknowledging "yes/indeed." It can stand alone or color another phrase: <b>pona a!</b> is "good, wow!" or "great!"

## What does "mani" mean?

Money, currency, or wealth &mdash; and, in some contexts, valuable livestock, echoing how many real languages' words for money trace back to cattle as an early store of value.

## This course has covered roughly 120 core words. Is that the entire language?

It is a widely shared foundation, commonly called <b>nimi pu</b> because the words are documented in the 2014 book <i>Toki Pona: The Language of Good</i>. That book presented <b>kin</b>, <b>namako</b>, and <b>oko</b> as synonyms of <b>a</b>, <b>sin</b>, and <b>lukin</b>; many speakers now distinguish them. The 2021 <i>Toki Pona Dictionary</i> (<b>ku</b>) recorded community usage rather than declaring a new mandatory vocabulary; <b>nimi ku suli</b> is a community label for 17 words that received especially broad use, such as <b>soko</b> for mushroom and <b>tonsi</b> for nonbinary or gender-diverse people. These words add convenient conventions, not concepts that were impossible to express before: for example, <b>lukin</b> already means "eye," while <b>oko</b> is another word some speakers use for it. Speakers vary in which words they use, and the language has no permanently closed master list.

## How could you describe a mushroom without using "soko"?

One concise option is <b>kili ma</b>, "earth/ground produce." You could add more detail for the situation; Toki Pona usually describes the relevant qualities instead of requiring one fixed word for every species or object.
